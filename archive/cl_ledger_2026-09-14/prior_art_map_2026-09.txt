Continual Learning, 2025–September 2026: State of the Art,
Bottlenecks, and a Prior-Art Map for the CRR Equanimity Rule
TL;DR
The ﬁeld has converged, empirically, on a single dominant lever —
replay/regularisation that keeps the model's output distribution close to its prior
distribution — and on a strong empirical predictor of forgetting (KL divergence to the
base model on the new task, "RL's Razor," Shenfeld et al., ICLR 2026), but essentially
every quantitative knob (replay fraction, loss weight λ , replay-batch size, memory-
bank update frequency) is hand-set, not derived. This is exactly the gap CRR's ratio-
based, unit-free quantities target.
Alex's "equanimity" rule (weight the mean replay gradient equal to the mean current-
batch gradient, Ω =1) has no exact prior art: the nearest neighbours are GradNorm
(multi-task gradient-norm equalisation, 2018), MEGA-I (loss-ratio balancing of
current vs. episodic-memory gradients, 2020), and GeRe's dynamic weight matching
replay loss magnitude to current-task loss (2025). All balance losses or equalise norms
across tasks; none states a closed-form weight-1 replay rule, and none reports the
speciﬁc "matches 100%-replay at ~40% less compute" result.
The path-length vs. endpoint prediction is genuinely under-tested. Current practice
measures endpoint KL / parameter displacement (RL's Razor; Bethune et al. scaling
laws) or bounds per-step forgetting (Loss-Adaptive Learning Rates, 2026), and there is
documented "drift-then-recover" oscillation in ﬁne-tuning — but no work uses
cumulative Fisher-Rao/KL trajectory length as the forgetting predictor, leaving CRR's
coherence-length hypothesis open.
Key Findings
1. Replay dominates at scale, but the mixing weight is heuristic. Across continual
pretraining (Ibrahim et al., 2024), LLM ﬁne-tuning (Marek et al., 2026; Kotha & Liang,
2026), and classic class-IL (DER++, Buzzega et al., 2020), replay is the workhorse.
Marek et al. use a ﬁxed replay weight λ =10 on a replay minibatch 4× smaller than the
downstream batch; DER/ER use ﬁxed per-benchmark α / β  on a separately-drawn
buffer minibatch; continual pretraining uses hand-set 5–50% replay fractions. None of
these weights is derived.
2. The forgetting "law" is empirical, not derived. RL's Razor (Shenfeld, Pari & Agrawal,
ICLR 2026, arXiv 2509.04259) shows forgetting collapses onto a single curve when
plotted against forward KL(base ǁ ﬁne-tuned) on the new task. The ﬁt is far tighter in a
controlled toy setting than in LLMs: "A quadratic ﬁt achieves R2 = 0.96 in this setting
[ParityMNIST]… The same correlation appears in our LLM experiments, with a
quadratic ﬁt achieving R2 = 0.71." On-policy RL is implicitly biased toward KL-
minimal solutions. The law itself (KL predicts forgetting) is ﬁtted; the "why RL stays
KL-small" part is derived.
3. Loss of plasticity has no consensus cause. Competing 2024–2026 accounts: unit
dormancy / lack of injected diversity (Dohare et al., Nature 2024, continual
backpropagation); Hessian/eNTK spectral collapse (He et al., ICML 2026);
dynamical-systems trapping near stable manifolds (Joudaki et al., ICLR 2026); and —
newly — abundant replay memory itself suppressing plasticity (Cho et al., "Forget
Forgetting," ICLR 2026). Each proposes a different ﬁx, which is itself evidence the
mechanism is unsettled.
4. Multi-timescale memory is back, with hand-set frequencies. Google's Nested
Learning / HOPE (Behrouz et al., arXiv 2512.24695) frames a model as nested
optimisation problems and a "Continuum Memory System" of memory banks each
updating at a different, hand-speciﬁed frequency — a direct architectural analogue of
CRR's occasions/resets, but with the timescales chosen by hand rather than derived
from the data's own resolvable steps.
5. Mechanistic localisation of forgetting is converging on middle/attention layers but
is contested. 2026 mechanistic work reports representational drift in intermediate
layers, gradient interference in attention weights, and MoE routing-gate drift; but the
ﬂagship arXiv 2601.18699 has hallmarks of an unreliable source (see Caveats).
Details
PART 1 — State of the Art (with mechanism, and whether the explanation is derived or
empirical)
Replay methods.
Experience Replay (ER) and Dark Experience Replay (DER/DER++) (Buzzega et al.,
NeurIPS 2020, arXiv 2004.07211). Mechanism (authors): interleaving buffered
exemplars — and, in DER, matching stored logits sampled along the optimisation
trajectory (knowledge distillation) — keeps the function from drifting on past data;
DER++ adds a ground-truth-label term to survive sudden distribution shift. 
DER uses reservoir sampling and needs no task boundaries. Empirical. The
buffer minibatch is drawn separately and its loss added with ﬁxed coefficients α  (logit-
matching) and β  (labels),  typically α =1, β =0.5, buffer minibatch ≈32.
CLS-ER (dual-memory experience replay, Arani et al., ICLR 2022): maintains fast- and
slow-updating semantic memories (EMA of weights) alongside the episodic buffer;
mechanism (authors) is the complementary-learning-systems analogy — a plastic
fast learner and a stable slow learner, consolidated by aligning decisions on buffered
samples. Empirical/analogical.
SuRe (Surprise-driven Prioritised Replay) (Hazard et al., 2025, arXiv 2511.22367):
prioritises replay of high-surprise (high prediction-error) prompts. Mechanism:
surprise proxies for samples most at risk of being overwritten; concentrating replay
there is sample-efficient. Empirical.
Self-generated / synthetic replay: the model samples its own training distribution
instead of storing exemplars. In LLMs this "nearly eliminates forgetting" (Marek et al.,

Mechanism (authors): self-samples approximate the pretraining distribution well

On-policy replay for continual SFT (arXiv 2605.29495, 2026): replaces stale gold
answers attached to historical prompts with reward-ﬁltered on-policy samples.
Mechanism: on-policy targets stay near the current policy, reducing distributional
drift (a corollary of RL's Razor). Empirical, motivated by theory.
GeRe (General-sample Replay) (Zhang et al., 2025, arXiv 2508.04676): a small, ﬁxed
pre-collected set of generic pretraining texts is replayed to retain general capability,
 with a threshold-based-margin (TM) activation-consistency loss. Mechanism
(authors): anchoring neural activation states on general samples preserves broad
capability and even improves downstream performance. Empirical. Notably,
GeRe uses Dynamic Weight Balancing: ω ^TM = detach(L^CE / L^TM) so the replay
loss magnitude is scaled to match the current-task CE loss  — the closest
published analogue to an "equanimity" idea, but on losses, not gradients.
Regularisation and distillation.
EWC family (Kirkpatrick et al., PNAS 2017) and Synaptic Intelligence (Zenke et al.,
2017): quadratic penalty on parameter change weighted by (Fisher-based)
importance. Mechanism (authors): protect parameters important to prior tasks.
Derived (Laplace/Fisher approximation) but the approximation is known to be loose;
RL's Razor reframes EWC as an approximate KL-minimisation.
Learning without Forgetting (Li & Hoiem, 2017): distil the old model's outputs on new-
task inputs. Mechanism: functional (output-space) anchoring. Empirical.
KL regularisation on replay data (Marek et al., 2026, arXiv 2605.26097): add
λ ·KL(base ǁ model) on replay/self-generated data; they ﬁnd the KL objective beats plain
NTP for retention,  and that λ =10 (verbatim, Fig. 4/6 captions: "a strong
regularization coefficient ( λ =10) to minimize forgetting") on a 4×-smaller replay
minibatch (Appendix B.2: "a batch size 4-times smaller than the downstream batch
(to reduce the computational overhead from regularization)") works well. Capacity
lower-bounds forgetting: a near-saturated (overtrained) model cannot learn without
overwriting. Empirical, with the KL-estimator derivation above. λ  and the batch
ratio are hand-set.
Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) / "Self-Distillation Enables Continual Learning"
(Shenfeld, Damani, Hübotter, Agrawal, 2026, arXiv 2601.19897; ICML 2026): use in-
context conditioning to turn a demonstration into a per-instance reward, enabling on-
policy learning from demonstrations. Mechanism (authors): on-policy distribution
alignment keeps updates in a trust region around the base policy → less forgetting
than off-policy SFT. Derived from an inverse-RL argument.

Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) / "Reinforcement Learning via Self-

RL's Razor (Shenfeld & Agrawal, ICLR 2026, arXiv 2509.04259): the empirical
forgetting law KL(base ǁ ﬁne-tuned) on the new task predicts forgetting; on-policy RL
is implicitly biased toward KL-minimal solutions among those solving the task. 
They ablate weight change, representation drift, rank/sparsity, reverse KL, total
variation, L2 — none explains forgetting as well as forward KL. Law is empirical
(R ² =0.96 toy / 0.71 LLM); the "why RL is KL-minimal" part is derived.
Critique — "Rethinking On-Policy Self-Distillation for Thinking Models" (arXiv
2607.05184): questions whether on-policy self-distillation retains its advantage on
long-chain-of-thought "thinking" models. [Flagged: I could not independently verify

EMA / dual-memory / slow–fast consolidation. Mean-teacher/EMA weight averaging and
CLS-ER (above) instantiate a stable slow copy and a plastic fast copy; "Forget Forgetting"
(Cho et al., ICLR 2026) uses weight averaging for stability + rank-based resets for plasticity.
Mechanism: temporal averaging suppresses high-frequency drift. Empirical/analogical.
Multi-timescale architectures.Nested Learning / HOPE / Titans continuum memory
(Behrouz et al., Google, arXiv 2512.24695): memory as a spectrum of banks, each updating
at a hand-set frequency;  HOPE adds self-modifying ("unbounded levels
of in-context learning") updates on top of Titans' surprise-based long-term memory.
 Mechanism (authors): separating timescales lets fast banks absorb novelty while
slow banks consolidate, reducing interference. Empirical (proof-of-concept), timescales
hand-set.
Continual pretraining of LLMs.
Ibrahim/Gupta et al. (TMLR 2024, arXiv 2403.08763): verbatim, "a simple and scalable
combination of learning rate (LR) re-warming, LR re-decaying, and replay of previous
data is sufficient to match the performance of fully re-training from scratch on all
available data… matching the re-training baseline using only a fraction of the
compute" — validated at 405M and 10B across weak (En→En) and strong (En→De)
shifts. Replay fraction is hand-set (small % suffices for weak shift; more for strong
shift). Empirical.
Kotha & Liang, "Replaying pre-training data improves ﬁne-tuning" (2026, arXiv
2603.04964): replaying generic data during ﬁne-tuning not only prevents forgetting
but improves the target task — up to 1.87× (ﬁne-tuning) / 2.06× (mid-training) data
efficiency; helps more when target data is scarce in pretraining. Empirical.
"The Finetuner's Fallacy" (Baek et al., 2026, arXiv 2603.16177): specialised pretraining
(repeating the domain set from pretraining) beats ﬁne-tuning-only; a 1B specialised-

"Sharpness-Aware Pretraining Mitigates Catastrophic Forgetting" (Watts et al., 2026,

Scaling laws for forgetting (Bethune et al., 2025, arXiv 2502.06042): forgetting under

uses endpoint cross-entropy-to-base as the forgetting metric. Derived scaling ﬁt
(empirical form).
Loss of plasticity.
Dohare et al. (Nature 632:768–774, 2024): standard deep nets lose plasticity in
continual settings — verbatim, "In ImageNet, binary classiﬁcation performance
dropped from 89% accuracy on an early task down to 77%, about the level of a linear
network, on the 2000th task." Continual backpropagation (reinitialise a small fraction
of least-used units) maintains plasticity. Mechanism (authors): sustained learning
needs a random, non-gradient diversity injection. Empirical.
"Spectral Collapse Drives Loss of Plasticity" (He et al., ICML 2026, arXiv 2509.22335):
plasticity loss is preceded by Hessian spectral collapse at new-task init; derives ε -rank
conditions and shows the loss-weighted Gram matrix is spectrally equivalent to the
Gauss–Newton approximation; ﬁx = maintain effective feature rank + L2. Derived.
Activation-function design for plasticity — deep Fourier features and
learnable/wavelet activations (2026, e.g. arXiv 2509.22562 and related):
structured/periodic features resist rank collapse. Empirical + partial theory. (Exact IDs
for the "deep Fourier features" and Aug-2026 wavelet papers not fully veriﬁed within
budget.)
"Forget Forgetting" (Cho et al., ICLR 2026, arXiv 2502.07274): in the abundant-
memory regime the bottleneck ﬂips from stability to plasticity — abundant exemplar
memory suppresses new-task learning because the expected update direction
collapses onto the span of past gradients as replay weight λ →0. Fix = Weight-Space
Consolidation (rank-based resets + weight averaging). Empirical with a short
gradient-geometry derivation.
Theory in scaling limits.
"The Importance of Being Lazy" (Graldi et al., ICML 2025, arXiv 2506.16884): using
DMFT, width helps only insofar as it induces laziness (less feature learning); a task-
similarity-modulated transition separates a lazy low-forgetting regime from a rich
high-forgetting regime; optimal performance at a critical level of feature learning.
Derived.
"Convergence of continual learning in homogeneous deep networks" (2026) and
related (Jung et al., ICLR 2025 on GD implicit bias in continual linear classiﬁcation;
Karpel et al., 2026, optimal L2 in high-dim continual linear regression, arXiv
2601.13844): characterise convergence/implicit bias in idealised settings. Derived.
Gradient-balancing / gradient-projection prior art (relevant to equanimity).
GEM / A-GEM (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, 2017; Chaudhry et al., 2019): project the current
gradient so it does not increase loss on buffered tasks. Derived (constrained QP).
MEGA-I/II (Guo et al., NeurIPS 2020, arXiv 1909.11763): unify GEM/A-GEM as
balancing two gradients;  MEGA-I sets mixing weights from the loss
ratio (if current loss is small, shift emphasis to memory);  MEGA-II rotates the
current gradient toward the reference. GEM/A-GEM are degenerate cases that always
weight the current task at 1. Derived optimisation view.
GradNorm (Chen et al., ICML 2018, arXiv 1711.02257): equalises per-task gradient
norms to the average gradient norm  — verbatim, "The
common scale for gradients is most naturally the average gradient norm, G_W(t)," and
GradNorm "directly tun[es] the gradients to equalize task training rates," controlled
by a single asymmetry hyperparameter α  ( α =0 → exactly equal norms). Multi-task, not
replay. Derived heuristic.
Mammoth's additive-loss ER: separate buffer minibatch, ﬁxed additive weight — see
DER above.
Benchmarks and evaluation. Mammoth (aimagelab) is the de-facto CL framework;
 standard splits are class-IL / task-IL / domain-IL, plus task-free and recurring
streams. "Continual evaluation / the stability gap" (De Lange, van de Ven & Tuytelaars,
ICLR 2023, arXiv 2205.13452) shows "severe forgetting of past tasks occurs already during
the ﬁrst few training iterations on a new task," but this forgetting "is temporary and
followed by a phase of performance recovery… we refer to [this] as the stability gap" — an
endpoint-vs-trajectory distinction directly relevant to CRR. "Continual learning should
move beyond incremental classiﬁcation" (2025) argues the ﬁeld's benchmarks are too
narrow. There is a NeurIPS 2026 workshop on test-time continual-learning agents.

Agent-memory infrastructure. Continuum-Memory-System deployments for LLM agents
(e.g., arXiv 2605.29055, 2601.13186) implement fast/medium/long memory layers with
explicit staleness/decay; these are engineering claims, largely un-benchmarked against CL
metrics.
PART 2 — Bottlenecks the ﬁeld names (consolidated)
1. The forgetting law is empirical/underived. RL's Razor ﬁts KL→forgetting (R²=0.96
toy, 0.71 LLM) but does not derive the functional form; it explicitly frames KL as a
"reliable predictor," not a ﬁrst-principles law.

2. Hand-set replay weight/loss coefficient. Marek et al. use λ =10 on a 4×-smaller replay
batch — both chosen, not derived. GeRe uses a detached loss-ratio. DER uses per-
benchmark α / β .
3. Hand-set replay fractions in continual pretraining. Ibrahim et al. use small % for
weak shift, more for strong shift; Finetuner's Fallacy tunes {0,10,20%}; surveys note
"5% vs 25% by shift strength." No derivation of the correct fraction.
4. Hand-set update frequencies in continuum-memory systems. Nested
Learning/HOPE's memory-bank frequencies are design choices.
5. No consensus cause of loss of plasticity (dormancy vs spectral collapse vs dynamical
trapping vs abundant-memory suppression).
6. Capacity measured only by proxy. Marek et al. show capacity lower-bounds
forgetting but proxy it via tokens-per-parameter / pretraining saturation, not a direct
measure. 
7. Single-task/short-sequence results may not extend to long multi-task streams
(widely acknowledged; e.g., mechanistic and post-training-forgetting papers test few
tasks).
8. Abundant memory reduces plasticity (Cho et al.) — a newly named trade-off.
9. Cost of on-policy sampling. SDPO/on-policy distillation require generating rollouts
(SDPO ~2× generations vs SFT for the self-teacher baseline); on-policy replay pays a
sampling tax.
10. Task-boundary detection in task-free streams remains unsolved (DER avoids
boundaries but general task-free CL is open).
11. Where forgetting localises is contested (middle layers / attention / MoE routing vs
function vectors).
12. Compute cost of replay at frontier scale. Continual pretraining saves vs full
retraining (Ibrahim et al.); Hickok (2025, arXiv 2505.12512) reports 55–65% fewer
replay samples for equal performance via reduced replay ratio + consolidation. Exact
frontier-scale FLOP ﬁgures for replay are rarely reported.
PART 3 — Prior-art check for the equanimity rule (honest)
(i) Set replay weight so replay gradient norm = current-task gradient norm /
balance mean gradients of old and new data.No exact match found. Near-misses:
GradNorm (equalises task gradient norms to the average, multi-task, learns weights
via auxiliary loss — not replay, not weight-1, not closed-form); MEGA-I (balances
current vs. episodic-memory gradients by loss ratio, adaptive, not norm-equality);
GeRe (dynamic weight matching replay loss magnitude to current CE loss via a
detached ratio). All balance losses or equalise norms across tasks; none states the
mean-replay-gradient = mean-current-gradient (weight-1) rule.
(ii) Replay minibatch size vs replay loss weight trade-off. Partially present but not as
a principled trade-off: Marek et al. use a 4×-smaller replay batch and λ =10 (they up-
weight the smaller batch), motivated only as "to reduce the computational overhead
arxiv
from regularization." DER draws a ﬁxed-size buffer minibatch (~32) with ﬁxed α / β . No
paper isolates "small batch, unit weight" as an optimum.
(iii) Additive-loss ER (separate means, weight 1) tolerates a much smaller replay
minibatch.Not found as an explicit claim. Mammoth/DER use additive separate-
minibatch losses but with tuned weights, not weight 1, and do not report tolerance to
shrinking the replay minibatch at unit weight.
(iv) Compute reduction from small-weighted replay.The speciﬁc "~40% less
compute, matches 100% replay" is not in the literature. Closest: Hickok (2025) — 55–
65% fewer replay samples for equal performance (via reduced replay ratio + a
consolidation phase, not a gradient-balancing weight). Marek et al. motivate the 4×-
smaller batch as compute-saving but give no % ﬁgure.
Verdict: The equanimity rule as Alex states it — a closed-form, unit-free, weight-1 balancing
of mean gradients of replay and current batches, plus the small-replay-batch compute result
— appears genuinely novel relative to GradNorm/MEGA/GeRe/DER. The strongest
overlapping ideas are conceptual (balance old vs new; equalise magnitudes), not identical.
PART 4 — Path-length vs endpoint forgetting
Endpoint predictors dominate. RL's Razor uses endpoint forward KL on the new task;
 Bethune et al. use endpoint cross-entropy-to-base; many use ǁ θ _T −
θ _0 ǁ . These are displacement/endpoint quantities.
Per-step (not cumulative-path) bounds exist. "Fine-Tuning Without Forgetting via
Loss-Adaptive Learning Rates" (2026, arXiv 2605.20005) bounds per-step forgetting
and notes bounding it "at each step directly controls cumulative forgetting" — the
closest to a path-integral view, but it bounds a sum of per-step losses, not Fisher-Rao
arc length.
Oscillation / "wasted travel" is documented but not costed as path length. The
stability-gap literature (De Lange et al., 2023) and "Overcoming Forgetting with
Evolution Strategies" (arXiv 2605.30148) show prior-task accuracy drops then
recovers, and that random-walk weight drift (larger ǁ θ _t− θ _0 ǁ ) tracks forgetting —
evidence that trajectories oscillate and that travel correlates with forgetting, but
nobody integrates arc length.
No work uses cumulative KL / cumulative Fisher distance / trajectory length as the
forgetting predictor. RL's Razor even shows per-step KL change anti-correlates with
gradient similarity (bigger steps → more forgetting), which is suggestive of a path-
length effect, but the paper's headline predictor remains endpoint KL.
Verdict: CRR's path-length (arc actually travelled) vs endpoint-displacement distinction is
an open, untested prediction. The cheapest decisive test: ﬁne-tune two ways to the same
endpoint KL but different cumulative trajectory length (e.g., high-LR-oscillatory vs low-LR-
direct), and check whether forgetting tracks cumulative Fisher-Rao length or endpoint KL.

Recommendations
1. Publish the equanimity rule now as a distinct contribution, positioning it explicitly
against GradNorm (norm-equalisation, multi-task), MEGA-I (loss-ratio, episodic
memory), and GeRe (loss-magnitude matching). The novelty is the gradient-mean
parity at unit weight, its LR/batch invariance, and the small-replay-batch compute
result — none of which the three have. Report the Fashion-MNIST counterexample
prominently; reviewers will trust the claim more.
2. Run the path-length test next — it is the cheapest high-value experiment and the one
prediction with no existing competitor. Fine-tune to matched endpoint KL along a
short-direct vs long-oscillatory path and regress forgetting on cumulative Fisher-Rao
length. If forgetting tracks arc length better than endpoint KL, that is a direct,
publishable refutation of the RL's-Razor endpoint predictor.
3. Re-analyse existing public datasets (RL's Razor release; Marek et al. code at
github.com/martin-marek/forgetting; Mammoth logs) with CRR quantities before
running new large models — you can test the ratio-invariance and coherence-length
claims on data already released.
4. Frame CRR against the "hand-set knob" bottleneck, which recurs in every subﬁeld
( λ , replay fraction, memory frequency, reset fraction). A framework that derives any
one of these from a unit-free coherence quantity is immediately useful; lead with
replay weight since you already have the equanimity result.
Benchmarks that would change these recommendations: if a 2026 paper appears that (a)
derives the replay weight from gradient parity, or (b) uses cumulative Fisher/KL length as a
forgetting predictor, the novelty claims weaken — monitor arXiv cs.LG for both. If the
equanimity optimum fails to hold on a third dataset family beyond Fashion-MNIST,
downgrade recommendation 1 to "robust heuristic" rather than "derived optimum."

The "~40% less compute" and "matches 100%-replay" equanimity results are
Alex's own ﬁndings; I found no external corroboration, and the nearest published
compute-reduction number is Hickok (2025)'s 55–65% fewer replay samples via a
different mechanism. This strengthens the novelty claim but means the result is
currently unreplicated externally.
Established vs claimed vs contested:Established — replay mitigates forgetting;
continual pretraining saves compute vs retraining; plasticity loss is real (Nature 2024).
Claimed — RL's Razor's KL law (strong evidence, single-curve ﬁts, but empirical); self-
generated replay "nearly eliminates" forgetting. Contested — the
mechanism/localisation of forgetting and the cause of plasticity loss.
