The electric grid has not historically experienced simultaneous load losses of this magnitude in response to a fault on the system, which has historically been planned for large generation losses but not for such significant simultaneous load losses.

- **Identifier:** NERC LLTF white paper dated July 2025 (PDF creation 2025-07-22),

> Generally, AI training executed in large clusters has rapid fluctuations during training periods and while saving checkpoint [...] progress. Additionally, the transition from training to saving checkpoint progress (or vice versa) may happen in under one second.

> In the fastest ramping period, the demand changes at a rate of 1.9 p.u. per second for about 250 milliseconds. While the training is active, jittery spikes up and down occur, shown between 360 and 660 seconds in Figure 2.2.

> Data center industry experts note that power delivery systems for HPC loads (AI data centers) commonly exclude UPS protection systems for the IT equipment. Instead, they employ a checkpoint process where checkpoints are saved at regular intervals. If there is an interruption to the IT equipment’s power, the checkpoint may be restored after the power interruption ends.

> For example, Figure 3.1 shows a North American data center ramping down from about 450 MW to about 40 MW within 36 seconds around hour 6. The load’s demand is constant (around 7 MW) for approximately 4 hours. After hour 10, the data center ramps back up to 450 MW over the course of a few minutes.

> In addition to the loss of load, the sudden restoration of a large load could exhaust available balancing reserves, ultimately leading to decreased system frequency and potential frequency instability.

> For example, AI model training at the xAI Colossus Supercomputer in Memphis, Tennessee—currently the world’s largest AI training cluster [...] —can change the loading 35–70 MW or more within a minute as the model starts and stops. That change may not be an issue for a single facility, but the aggregate effect across multiple facilities may negatively impact ACE control.

> These loads are typically not controllable by the utility or ISO unless they voluntarily enter into a load curtailment or demand-response program.

> Concepts have been explored [...] in some areas around mandatory load curtailments by the ISO or utility during stressful grid conditions, which might allow for TPs to assume a lower peak demand for the load and potentially reduce the transmission buildout exclusively needed to support the load.

(The "[...]" in the first quote omits footnotes 10–14 and a page header; in the sixth the footnote mark 29; in the last the
footnote mark 23.)

**Reading.**
- [G5 states] Start/stop of AI training moves tens of MW within a minute, a data centre dropped about 410 MW in 36 s and
  came back over minutes, and "sudden restoration of a large load" is named as a frequency risk; the aggregate across
  facilities is the stated concern. This is G5 (ramps and restoration) as a grid-reliability position.
- [G6 bears] The transition into checkpoint saving may happen in under one second, and experts note that AI data centres
  commonly exclude UPS protection for the IT equipment, relying on periodic checkpoints. Two inferences are the
  investigator's, not the source's: the save that a lossless pause needs before the power drops is itself a grid-visible
  power step, and a power loss without a save loses the work since the last checkpoint.
- [G1 bears] Large loads (computational, industrial, hydrogen) are "typically not controllable" by the utility or ISO
  unless they voluntarily join a curtailment or demand-response programme: participation is possible and voluntary; the
  passage does not say that AI training takes part.
- [G3 close] "the checkpoint may be restored after the power interruption ends" is checkpoint-and-resume as the
  continuity mechanism, but for unplanned interruptions and with no statement of the cost.
- [G8 close] Mandatory curtailment "might allow" a lower planned peak and less transmission build: flexibility as an
  interconnection lever, stated as an explored concept, not as the main commercial value.
- Not supplied: any AI-training demand-response event, any price, any rebound after a DR event.

> Regulation Service is capacity that can be deployed by ERCOT systems every 4 seconds to balance supply with demand in between the 5-min Security-Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED) intervals and maintain frequency close to 60 Hz.

> RRS-FFR is (full) response when frequency is below 59.85 Hz within 250 ms

> Some Large Loads, for example crypto-mining data centers, can quickly change consumption based on changes in ERCOT wholesale prices.   investigator's.
- [G5 states; G5 contradicts in part] Uncoordinated simultaneous changes by many price-responsive large loads are named as
  a frequency risk, but ERCOT reports no observed reliability problem from large-load ramps through mid-2024 and planned no
  change. G5 holds as a stated risk; as an observed harm (for ramps, as distinct from fault-triggered trips) it is
  qualified.
- Public data (not quoted; seen in the extracted text): Appendix 2 lists RRS and ECRS release events (start, end,
  duration, maximum MW released) and off-line Non-Spin deployments, a small event table readable from the PDF.

### Li, "Beyond Scalar Flexibility: From Eligible AI Workloads to Dependable Load Relief"

> Load relief is a power service rather than an energy saving. If every deferred megawatt-hour rebounds and the expected lost work equals half of a one-hour checkpoint interval, net energy change after a four-hour call is −12.5% of gross curtailed energy. For the full-realization 2.32 MW offer, that rebound contains 10.44 MWh. The mean noneligible workload power, defined as the floor plus shift layers, is 23.264 MW; absorbing the rebound at 10% of that level takes 4.49 hours. This rule implies 8.49 hours between four-hour calls and at most 19.8 calls per week.

> The shift layer is a semantic pool, not an offered capacity, because a running job may require checkpoint, migration, or restart.

**Reading.**
- [G5 bears] The paper's accounting assumes that every deferred megawatt-hour rebounds ("If every deferred megawatt-hour
  rebounds"); under that assumption, absorbing the rebound at 10% of the non-eligible load takes 4.49 hours and limits
  calls to at most 19.8 a week. This is the rebound as energy to be absorbed rather than as a peak; both the full rebound
  and the 10% rate are the paper's assumptions, not observations.
- [G3 bears] The paper assumes lost work of half a one-hour checkpoint interval per call, i.e. a pause that is not
  lossless; the declaration's G3 lossless pause removes that term. The difference is in an assumption, not an empirical
  finding either way, so it neither supports nor contradicts G3.
- [G6 bears] "may require checkpoint, migration, or restart": the checkpoint is the named precondition of shifting.

> In the experiment shown in the top plot, the throttling signals are released simultaneously at 12.00 h, i.e., ∆T = 0, which results in a sharp load ramp and a peak rebound of 64.8 kW.   loads are heat pumps (74 buildings in the second experiment), not compute.

### Michaels Energy for the Minnesota Department of Commerce, "Demand Response Impact Study" (May 1, 2013)

- **Identifier:** report dated May 1, 2013 (PDF modified 2021-12-04),
  https://michaelsenergy.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/DR-Snapback-Report.pdf, fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 bc0814d1ac4cd2c27c16a6803eb3cd50821d0c913d366cf02b26aba81e63b1c7.
- **Local copy:** `michaels_dr_snapback.pdf` (text `michaels_dr_snapback.pdf.txt`)
- **Positions:** G5.

> Snapback is the increase in energy and demand in the hours immediately following a demand response event.

> The technologies used for demand response that exhibit snapback are: air conditioner cycling, water heater curtailment, and electric heating cycling. Other technologies that are often used do not have snapback effects due to the nature of their operations. These include ice storage, electric heating thermal storage, and on-site generation.

**Reading.**
- [G5 bears] The study defines snapback as the increase in energy and demand in the hours after a DR event, finds it in
  air-conditioner cycling, water-heater curtailment and electric-heating cycling, and finds none in ice storage, thermal
  storage and on-site generation "due to the nature of their operations". The source does not discuss compute loads. The
  investigator's reading, not the source's: the loads with snapback are ones that must recover a thermal state, while a
  paused training job returns to its baseline draw and its deferred work extends the run in time, so for AI load the
  grid-side risk may be the synchronized return ramp to baseline rather than demand above baseline, unless the scheduler
  runs extra jobs to catch up; Chen et al. (above) assert the opposite possibility, secondary peaks from deferred AI tasks
  rescheduled together. The declaration's H5 wording ("the rebound peak equals the curtailed load") is consistent with the
  investigator's reading if "peak" is read as the size of the return step.
