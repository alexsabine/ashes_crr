# DR1 citation checks, family F4: the engineering of pausing training at scale (checkpoint save and restore, restart overheads, pre-emption notices, power capping) (2026-09-29; R10)

Sources for DR1's graded sweep (`Grid_Demand_Response/DECLARATION.md` §1, pushed at e67e8ee), family F4: how long a large
training job takes to save and restore its state, what a restart costs beyond the save (job start-up, NCCL initialisation,
data-loader state, lost work), how much warning an interrupted job gets in practice, and power capping or GPU frequency
throttling as an alternative to pausing. F4 is graded mainly on positions **G3** (a lossless pause, checkpoint and resume
with nothing lost, makes the job's curtailment cost about the restart overhead), **G6** (a lossless pause needs a
checkpoint save before the power drops, which limits the demand-response products it can meet) and **G9** (power capping
or throttling, running slower, is an alternative to pausing, with different energy and grid effects). Where a source also
bears on G1, G2 or G5, that is tagged too. Fetched with curl through the session's HTTPS proxy (TLS verified throughout; no

## 1. Checkpoint save and restore at scale (G3, G6)

### Llama Team, AI @ Meta, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models"

- **Identifier:** arXiv:2407.21783, current version **v3** (23 Nov 2024; v1 submitted 31 Jul 2024), abstract page and PDF
  fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF (v3) sha256 481f1599468f95a07d05e97fafe55bbe786dc1624c6f881bcb4c7d14c933d083; abstract page sha256
  f8727697d2a3c676bd75b69327f6acc75a4bf0b6cc4f4d0936f2096b1ff78b64.
- **Local copy:** `pdf_2407.21783v3.pdf` (text `pdf_2407.21783v3.pdf.txt`)
- **Positions:** G3, G6, G5.

> A major challenge is supporting the highly bursty checkpoint writes that saturate the storage fabric for short durations. Checkpointing saves each GPU’s model state, ranging from 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU, for recovery and debugging. We aim to minimize GPU pause time during checkpointing and increase checkpoint frequency to reduce the amount of lost work after a recovery.

> Moreover, the synchronous nature of training makes it less fault-tolerant—a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job. Despite these challenges, for Llama 3, we achieved higher than 90% effective training time while supporting automated cluster maintenance, such as firmware and Linux kernel upgrades (Vigraham and Leonhardi, 2024), which resulted in at least one training interruption daily.

> During a 54-day snapshot period of pre-training, we experienced a total of 466 job interruptions. Of these, 47 were planned interruptions due to automated maintenance operations such as firmware upgrades or operator- initiated operations like configuration or dataset updates.

> To increase the effective training time, we reduced job startup and checkpointing time, and developed tools for fast diagnosis and problem resolution.

> During training, tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time, for example, due to all GPUs waiting for checkpointing or collective communications to finish, or the startup or shutdown of the entire training job. When this happens, it can result in instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid.

**Reading.**
- [G3 bears] Planned pauses of a frontier training job are routine: 47 planned interruptions in 54 days (466
  interruptions in all), and the paper says maintenance "resulted in at least one training interruption daily"; effective
  training time stayed above 90 % despite the interruptions. The paper names start-up time and checkpointing time as the
  costs it reduced, which matches G3's cost structure (a pause costs its save and its restart). The quoted passages give
  no per-interruption seconds, no split between planned and unplanned costs, and nothing about grid events.
- [G6 bears] The model state per GPU is 1 MB to 4 GB, and the checkpoint writes are "highly bursty" and saturate the
  storage fabric. So the save time is limited by the storage fabric's bandwidth. No save duration is given.
- [G5 states, the swings clause only] (outside F4's main positions) The whole job's start-up or shutdown, or all GPUs
  waiting for a checkpoint, swings the data centre's power by tens of megawatts. A pause and resume of a large job is
  therefore itself a ramp on the grid side, whatever it costs the job (G3 is about the job's cost, not the grid's). The
  quote states G5's synchronized ramps and power swings; it does not state the rebound clause (paused jobs resuming
  together).

### Gemini Team, Google, "Gemini: A Family of Highly Capable Multimodal Models"

- **Identifier:** arXiv:2312.11805, current version **v5** (9 May 2025; v1 19 Dec 2023), fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF (v5) sha256 8ca95b27851b09c335fa2abe9b84cec5848195a5914eec7fb0b635ba2d8fe4fe; abstract page sha256
  4445178534689d57de83a0327ae1350445b55664cb169225a7ea7e05fe3fae47.
- **Local copy:** `pdf_2312.11805v5.pdf` (text `pdf_2312.11805v5.pdf.txt`)
- **Positions:** G3, G6.

> Maintaining a high goodput2 at this scale would have been impossible using the conventional approach of periodic checkpointing of weights to persistent cluster storage. For Gemini models, we instead made use of redundant in-memory copies of the model state, and on any unplanned hardware failures, we rapidly recover directly from an intact model replica. Compared to both PaLM and PaLM-2 (Anil et al., 2023), this provided a substantial speedup in recovery time, despite the significantly larger training resources being used. As a result, the overall goodput for the largest-scale training job increased from 85% to 97%.

> We define goodput as the time spent computing useful new steps over the elapsed time of the training job.

**Reading.**
- [G3 bears] The "2" after "goodput" is a footnote mark (the second quote is that footnote). For unplanned hardware
  failures, recovery from in-memory replicas rather than periodic checkpoints on persistent storage is credited with
  raising goodput from 85 % to 97 %. So how the state is recovered is a large part of the interruption cost at this
  scale, and a design that keeps the state live cuts it sharply. The source is about unplanned failures, not planned
  pauses.
- [G6 bears] In-memory replicas need the machines holding them to stay powered. A demand-response pause that powers down
  the machines holding the replicas cannot use this route; the quote does not say whether the replicas sit in accelerator
  or host memory, so whether powering down only the accelerators is compatible is not settled here. The quoted passages
  do not discuss power-down pauses. No seconds are given.

### Wang et al., "GEMINI: Fast Failure Recovery in Distributed Training with In-Memory Checkpoints" (SOSP '23)

- **Identifier:** SOSP '23, October 23–26, 2023, Koblenz, Germany (Rice University and Amazon Web Services); author copy at
  https://www.cs.rice.edu/~eugeneng/papers/SOSP23.pdf, fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 d0185dfccd5a620e17a13f67ff02b6c658a908d873cf516058680aaab8b03687.
- **Local copy:** `gemini_sosp23.pdf` (text `gemini_sosp23.pdf.txt`)
- **Positions:** G6, G3.

> However, existing solutions are restricted by the low bandwidth to remote persistent storage, resulting in significant failure recovery costs, i.e., taking up to tens of minutes to retrieve the checkpoint captured a few hours ago to resume the training.

> For example, it takes 42 minutes to checkpoint the model states of MT-NLG [68] to the remote persistent storage when the bandwidth is 20Gbps.

> Compared to existing solutions [3, 48], Gemini reduces the checkpoint retrieval time by up to 250× and improves the checkpoint frequency by up to 8×. As a result, Gemini achieves a faster failure recovery by more than 13× without incurring over- head on training throughput.

> We define wasted time as the sum of the time spent on the lost training process before a failure and the time for retrieving the latest checkpoint during a failure recovery.

**Reading.**
- [G6 bears] A full save of a very large model (MT-NLG; the title of the paper's reference [68] is "Using DeepSpeed and Megatron to train Megatron-Turing NLG 530B") to remote storage
  at 20 Gbps takes 42 minutes. Restoring from remote storage takes "up to tens of minutes". If a lossless grid pause had
  to persist a model of that size to remote storage before the power dropped, it could not meet any product with a
  response time under 42 minutes on that storage. The number is for one storage bandwidth. It is not a demand-response
  measurement. The paper's in-memory design cuts checkpoint retrieval time by up to 250× and failure recovery by more
  than 13×; the quotes give no reduced figure for a save to persistent storage, which is what a power-down pause would
  need.
- [G3 close] The paper's "wasted time" (lost progress plus retrieval, with checkpoint time added in its eq. 1, re-typed:
  T_wasted = t_ckpt + 1/(2f) + t_rtvl) is the per-interruption cost of an unplanned failure. For a planned pause with a
  checkpoint at the pause, the lost-progress term is zero, leaving save plus retrieval. That is G3's "about the restart
  overhead", in the domain's own terms. The paper is about failures, not planned pauses.

  USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation, April 16–18, 2024, from
  https://www.usenix.org/system/files/nsdi24-jiang-ziheng.pdf; both fetched 2026-09-29. Quotes are from the USENIX PDF.
- **Integrity:** USENIX PDF sha256 a998454d5a1554f4c3c56577f4a25cc8eea96a6968231564a5b4f84a2ffd07d2; arXiv PDF (v1)
  sha256 4a04bff09d7cd937271662e8ca9a97f0d41eac9be1d35c9c8b2f18f2dc59ad20; abstract page sha256
  db214641adda41b31b4c38dab4bce7505e4fc44bc34a58040fc9381fb21d961f.
- **Local copy:** `megascale_nsdi24.pdf` (text `megascale_nsdi24.pdf.txt`)
- **Positions:** G3, G6.

> We conduct experiments on the same AI cluster in §6 and our empirical measurement indicates that the initialization time for Megatron-LM on 2,048 NVIDIA Ampere GPUs is approximately 1047 seconds. While this may appear relatively small compared to the training duration, it imposes a significant hurdle to routine testing and iterative development (e.g., minor code adjustments in hyperparameter tuning and debugging). It also hampers the implementation of fast restart-and-recovery mechanisms.

> This reduces the initialization time to 361 seconds on 2,048 GPUs.

> The initialization time is reduced to under 5 seconds on 2048 GPUs, and to under 30 seconds on more than 10,000 GPUs with those optimizations.

> In the first stage, each GPU worker writes its on-chip states to the host memory, and then continues the training process. After the optimization of Pytorch’s serialization mechanism and the use of pinned memory, this process can be reduced to several seconds thanks to the high PCIe bandwidth, thereby minimally interrupting the ongoing training process. In the second stage, a background process takes over, asynchronously transferring the state from the host memory to a distributed file system

> In the context of recovery from a checkpoint, it is on the critical path since training can not be started without the last checkpoint.

> Moreover, the system can catch up to the training progress prior to the crash within 15 minutes from the latest checkpoints, maintaining over 90% effective training time rate

**Reading.**
- [G3 bears] One component of the restart overhead, NCCL communication-group initialisation, measured 1047 s (about
  17 minutes) on 2,048 GPUs with default tooling. It fell to 361 s and then to under 5 s (under 30 s above 10,000 GPUs)
  after engineering. So the "restart overhead" R is not a constant of nature. It ranges over more than two orders of
  magnitude depending on the stack. Recovery (load plus catch-up) after a crash is "within 15 minutes".
- [G6 bears] A two-stage save needs the GPUs for only "several seconds" (device to host memory). The slow write to the
  distributed file system runs in the background. If the hosts stay powered through the pause, the GPU part of the save
  before the power drops is seconds, not minutes. G6 then binds only for products with response times of seconds, or for
  pauses that cut power to the hosts. This narrows G6 as written ("needs a checkpoint save before the power drops") without
  refuting it: if the hosts stay powered, the part that must precede the GPU power drop is short. (The label was
  "qualifies" in the first draft, which is not one of the four declared readings; see "Independent verification".)
- Not supplied: any grid event, any planned pause.

- [G6 bears] The numbers separate the three times that matter for a grid pause:

- [G3 bears] The data-loader state (about 1 GB, about 8 s to collect) is part of what a lossless pause must save. This

  https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast21-mohan.pdf, fetched 2026-09-29.

> Since model weights are constantly updated between iterations, checkpointing requires the training to brieﬂy pause to capture the model weights accurately. We term this overhead (i.e, the time GPU is idle, waiting for the checkpoint to complete) as the checkpoint stall.

> Our evaluation across a variety of models, GPUs, and storage types conﬁrms that CheckFreq reduces the wasted GPU time from order of hours to just under a minute, while incurring less than 3.5% runtime overhead, as compared to the existing epoch-based checkpointing schemes.

**Reading.**
- [G3 bears] The paper names the two ingredients a "lossless" pause needs:

  not frontier LLMs.
- [G6 bears] The checkpoint stall is the GPU-idle time before training can continue. It is the minimum that must precede
  a pause.
