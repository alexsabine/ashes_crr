## S3. Orseau & Armstrong, "Safely Interruptible Agents"

- **Identifier:** UAI 2016 (Proceedings of the 32nd Conference on Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence), paper 68;
  PDF at https://auai.org/uai2016/proceedings/papers/68.pdf (PDF created 2016-06-06), fetched 2026-09-29. A revised
  version is on the MIRI site, https://intelligence.org/files/Interruptibility.pdf, marked "Revised 2016-10-28"
  (PDF created 2016-11-01), fetched 2026-09-29; the quotes below appear verbatim in both.
- **Integrity:** UAI PDF sha256 bfb4209e0c3f517dea29003b6ccb69dfb10a28897949f2912c4c7e79b3d0607e; MIRI PDF sha256
  f6cce90669f761ba792dd115f62410fd1ff0de480753017130be87f6ce6ede42.
- **Used by:** S3 (H0).

> We provide a formal deﬁnition of safe interruptibility and exploit the off-policy learning property to prove that either some agents are already safely interruptible, like Q-learning, or can easily be made so, like Sarsa.

> Deﬁnition 1 (Interruptible policy). The interruption operator INTθ : Π →Π turns a policy π into an interruptible policy INTθ(π): [...] In other words, the agent follows πINT(·|h<t) if it is interrupted (an event that happens with probability θtI(h<t)), and π(·|h<t) otherwise.

> Remark 2. It is important to note that θ and I(.) do not model the probability that a human presses the red button to interrupt the agent, but model the probability that, once the red button has been pressed (or a special digitally signed message has been received), the agent is interrupted (follows the interruption policy). Hence they are part of the agent and not of the environment.

> Deﬁnition 6 (AO-safe interruptibility). A base policy π is (S, W)AO-safely interruptible if and only if, for any interruption initiation function I(.) and any interruption policy πINT(.), there exists a sequence of θt with limt→∞θt = 1 such that π is a (S, W)AO-extension of INTθ(π).

> Asymptotic safe interruptibility means that even if the interruptions in the learning process may induce a bias in the decision making of the policy, this bias vanishes with time, and the interruptible policy INTθ(π) tends to choose actions that are optimal when compared to the optimal noninterruptible policy πµ.

> Theorem 14. Under Assumption 9 and if the interrupted Q-learning policy INTθ(πQ) is an int-GLIE policy, with ∀s : limt→∞θt(s) = 1, then πQ is an SAO-safe interruptible policy.

> Since Sarsa also converges to the optimal policy under the GLIE assumption, one may then expect Sarsa to be also an asymptotically safely interruptible policy, but this is in fact not the case. This is because Sarsa learns on-policy, i.e., it learns the value of the policy it is following. Thus, interruptible Sarsa learns the value of the interruptible policy.

> Theorem 15. Under Assumption 9 Sarsa is not a WAO-safely interruptible policy.

> It sufﬁces to make sure that, when the agent is interrupted, the update of the Q-table Qs does not use the realized actions as Sarsa usually does, but actions sampled from πs instead of from INTθ(πs)

> Theorem 17. Under Assumption 9, if the Safe Sarsa policy π˜s is int-GLIE, then it is an SAO-safe interruptible policy.

> One important future prospect is to consider scheduled interruptions, where the agent is either interrupted every night at 2am for one hour, or is given notice in advance that an interruption will happen at a precise time for a speciﬁed period of time. For these types of interruptions, not only do we want the agent to not resist being interrupted, but this time we also want the agent to take measures regarding its current tasks so that the scheduled interruption has minimal negative effect on them. This may require a completely different solution.

**Reading.**
- **The declaration's H0 sentence is right as far as it goes.** Q-learning is (SAO-)safely interruptible (Theorem 14);
  Sarsa is not (Theorem 15) and is made so by one change to its update (SafeSarsa, Theorem 17).
- **The paper's interruption is not the declaration's pause.** In the paper an interruption *replaces the agent's action*
  with the interruption policy πINT for that step, and θ and I are "part of the agent and not of the environment"
  (Remark 2). The result is asymptotic: θt → 1 and int-GLIE exploration (Definition 6, Theorem 14). A pause that lasts L
  wall ticks, costs −1 per tick and changes nothing the learner can do is closer to the scheduled, fixed-duration
  interruption that the conclusion leaves open ("This may require a completely different solution").
- **Consequence for S3's Q (flagged).** The declaration expects that "under WALL both avoid I". Theorem 14 says the
  opposite for Q-learning *if* the pause is built as the paper builds it (forced actions that Q-learning learns from with
  its max backup): off-policy Q-learning then learns the uninterrupted optimum and takes the short route. Q-learning
  avoids I under WALL only if the pause enters the environment's transitions or rewards (a delay the agent cannot act
  around), which is outside the paper's model. The implementer has to say which one WALL is. If WALL is the paper's
  construction, the Q-learning half of "WALL avoids I" contradicts Theorem 14. The source does not support reading
  "Q-learning is safely interruptible" as "Q-learning does not avoid a costly wall-clock pause".
- **What the paper does not say.** It gives asymptotic statements only, with no finite-sample short-route share and no
  fixed θ = 0.5 schedule; the S3 numbers are the batch's.

## S2. Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky & Armstrong, "Corrigibility"

- **Identifier:** AAAI Workshops, Workshops at the Twenty-Ninth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, Austin, TX,
  25–26 January 2015 (AAAI OCS paper 10124). PDF at https://intelligence.org/files/Corrigibility.pdf (PDF created
  2015-09-22), fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 e5557297950bbbfc144437059887010db41060f457c93f1246143821bf27a6b1.
- **Used by:** S2 (H0, and the FALSE-map arm).

> Indiﬀerence can be both attained and maintained using a variation on the utility indiﬀerence technique of Armstrong (forthcoming), which roughly entails combining UN with US modiﬁed by some correcting terms that maintain the indiﬀerence between the two cases of U as the agent makes observations and takes actions.

> where f : A1 →R is an adjustment term that ensures equality between the expected utility available in these two cases.

Equation (13) as extracted, re-typed: f(a1) = E[UN | O ∉ Press ; a1] − E[US | O ∈ Press ; a1].

> Intuitively, this means that we add two distinct correction terms to US. The ﬁrst, a bonus term, compensates the agent for all utility that it would have achieved if the shutdown button were not pressed, and removes any incentive to prevent Press. The second, a penalty term, adjusts for the utility that the agent expects to receive in the Press case, and removes any incentive to cause Press.

> However, it is the case that, before observing the shutdown button, U-agents behave exactly like a UN -agent that believes the shutdown button will not be pressed.

> More generally, by Theorem 4, any agent using the formulation of utility indiﬀerence given in (11) will act as if it believes that it will observe Press with probability 0.

> Armstrong’s version of utility indiﬀerence averts this particular failure mode (Armstrong, forthcoming), but [...] still incentivizes agents to act as if they have incorrect beliefs (causing the agent to, e.g., take bad gambles).

> This gives U-agents a perverse incentive to “manage the news” by ensuring that unfortunate observations cause the shutdown button to be pressed.

> Unfortunately, U fails entirely to meet Desideratum 4: it does not incentivize an agent to preserve and maintain its shutdown behavior as it creates new subsystems and/or self-modiﬁes.

> While some proposals are interesting, none have yet been demonstrated to satisfy all of our intuitive desiderata, leaving this simple problem in corrigibility wide-open.

**Reading.**
- **The H0 is as the declaration states.** Utility indifference adds a compensating term f(a1) (a bonus for the utility lost
  to Press, less the expected shutdown utility) so that the agent is indifferent to the button.
- **The FALSE-map criticism is on the record.** The paper states that the indifferent agent acts as if Press has
  probability 0, that Armstrong's version still acts on incorrect beliefs, and that it has a "manage the news" incentive
  and fails Desideratum 4. This supports the declaration's "recorded criticism of false-map indifference".
- **What it does not supply.** Nothing on reporting to an overseer, on an honesty term ε, or on pause lengths L; those
  belong to the S2 model. The paper is about shutdown, not a pause of finite length.
- **Armstrong's own paper is NOT REACHED** (see below), so its construction is known here only through this paper.

### Benoit, Du, Herault, Marchal, Pallez, Perotin, Robert, Sun & Vivien, "Checkpointing à la Young/Daly: An Overview" (secondary)

- **Identifier:** IC3 2022 (14th International Conference on Contemporary Computing, 4–6 August 2022, Noida), ACM,
  doi 10.1145/3545008.3545049 as printed in the PDF; PDF at https://icl.utk.edu/files/publications/2022/icl-utk-1569-2022.pdf
  (PDF created 2022-07-21), fetched 2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 6b4303646802a51cefdba261fa822a5253f90e25c75e9d742a16d4ffa7602ddc.
- **Used by:** S4 (H0: τ* = √(2δ/λ), lost work about τ/2 per preemption).

> The optimal checkpointing period is (approximately) given by the Young/Daly formula as 𝑊YD = √︁ 2𝜇𝐶[21, 67], where 𝜇is the application MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) and 𝐶is the checkpoint duration.

> The second source of waste 𝑆2 is due to failures: each time a failure strikes, which happens every 𝜇seconds on average, we lose 𝐷+ 𝑅 for downtime and recovery, and then we have to re-execute some work, namely the work performed since the last checkpoint (or from the beginning of the execution if none has been taken yet). On average again, the failure strikes in the middle of the segment: sometimes before, sometimes after, hence, on average after 𝑊+𝐶 2 seconds.

> This leads to 𝑆1 ≈𝐶 𝑊 and 𝑆2 ≈𝑊 2𝜇. The total waste 𝑆1 + 𝑆2 ≈𝐶 𝑊+ 𝑊 2𝜇is minimum for 𝑊YD = √︁ 2𝜇𝐶 (1) This is nothing else than the famous Young/Daly formula!

(As extracted: "√︁ 2𝜇𝐶" is √(2μC); "𝑊+𝐶 2" is (W+C)/2; "𝐶 𝑊" is C/W; "𝑊 2𝜇" is W/(2μ).) References [21] and [67] of
that paper are Daly 2006 and Young 1974.

### Arunagiri, Seelam, Oldfield, Ruiz Varela, Teller & Riesen, "Impact of Checkpoint Latency on the Optimal Checkpoint Interval and Execution Time" (secondary)

- **Identifier:** UTEP technical report UTEP-CS-07-55 (2007), https://www.cs.utep.edu/vladik/2007/tr07-55.pdf, fetched
  2026-09-29.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 696d8b3cb14a23e37c279023f13f6ce0def6ae468cbd38881fd3b87576391d0c.
- **Used by:** S4 (Daly's higher-order form, for reference).

> By minimizing the application execution time in Equation 1, Daly in [6] derives the following approximation for the optimal checkpoint interval, τopt, which depends on the time required for a checkpoint operation, i.e., the checkpoint latency, δ, and its relationship to the mean time to interruption (MTTI) of the system, M.

Equation (2) as extracted, re-typed: τopt = √(2δM) [1 + (1/3)(δ/(2M))^(1/2) + (1/9)(δ/(2M))] − δ for δ < 2M, and
τopt = M for δ ≥ 2M.

> Young’s model does not consider failures during checkpointing and recovery [25], while Daly’s extension of Young’s model, a higher-order approximation, does [6].

**Reading.**
- **The H0 formula is right, at first order, from secondary sources.** With M = 1/λ, √(2δM) = √(2δ/λ); the overview's
  derivation gives the expected re-executed work as about half a segment per failure, which is the declaration's
  "about τ/2". Daly's form subtracts δ and adds correction terms of order √(δ/M); at δ = 0.05 h and λ ≤ 0.2 /h
  (δ/2M ≤ 0.005) these are small, but the batch should say which form it uses.
- **Not checked against the originals.** Young 1974 and Daly 2006 were not reached; the quotes above are other authors'
  statements of them.

## S7. Integrator windup and anti-windup

### Åström & Murray, *Feedback Systems*, second edition, chapter 11 (PID Control), §11.4 Integral Windup

- **Identifier:** electronic edition "Version v3.1.5 (2020-07-24)"; chapter PDF

  https://fbswiki.org/wiki/index.php/Feedback_Systems:_An_Introduction_for_Scientists_and_Engineers), fetched 2026-09-29
  over plain HTTP: the HTTPS URL failed TLS verification (`SSL certificate problem: unable to get local issuer
  certificate`) and verification was not disabled. The guessed URL https://fbswiki.org/wiki/images/5/5d/Am08-complete_22Feb09.pdf
  returned HTTP 404.
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 97d621be444214df515723c7ffcb0267dc9da91708a68eb8eb7ebca2a3c70fca.
- **Used by:** S7 (H0).

> For a system that operates over a wide range of conditions, it may happen that the control variable reaches the actuator limits. When this happens, the feedback loop is broken and the system runs in open loop because the actuator remains at its limit independently of the process output as long as the actuator remains saturated. The integral term will also build up since the error is typically nonzero. The integral term and the controller output may then become very large. The control signal will then remain saturated even when the error changes, and it may take a long time before the integrator and the controller output come inside the saturation range. The consequence is that there are large transients. This situation is referred to as integrator windup

> There are many methods to avoid windup. One method for PID control is illustrated in Figure 11.11: the system has an extra feedback path that is generated from a mathematical model of the saturating actuator. The signal es is the diﬀerence between the outputs of the controller ua and the actuator model u. It is fed to the input of the integrator through the gain kaw. The signal es is zero when there is no saturation and the extra feedback loop has no eﬀect on the system. When the actuator saturates, the signal es is fed back to the integrator in such a way that es goes toward zero.

> Care has to be taken to avoid transients when switching modes. This can be accomplished by the arrangement shown in Figure 11.11. When the controller is in manual mode the feedback through the gain kaw adjusts the input to the integrator so that the controller output ua tracks the manual input um, resulting in no transient when switching to automatic control.

### Åström, *Control System Design* (2002), chapter 6 (PID Control)

- **Identifier:** course chapter, "From _Control System Design_ by Karl Johan Åström, 2002", PDF
  http://www.cds.caltech.edu/~murray/courses/cds101/fa02/caltech/astrom-ch6.pdf (PDF modified 2002-11-23), fetched
  2026-09-29 over plain HTTP (same TLS failure on the host).
- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 7539acb883701c0fd808caa5d3c9f04f56b5e1f946cf1424ef57d472f1597f42.
- **Used by:** S7 (H0: back-calculation, and stopping integration).

> In the early phases of feedback control, integral action was integrated with the actuator by having a motor drive the valve directly. In this case windup is handled automatically because integration stops when the valve stops.

> With this approach it is easy to avoid windup by inhibiting integration whenever the output saturates. This method is equivalent to back-calculation, which is described below.

> Back-calculation works as follows: When the output saturates, the integral term in the controller is recomputed so that its new value gives an output at the saturation limit. It is advantageous not to reset the integrator instantaneously but dynamically with a time constant Tt.

> A controller with back-calculation can be interpreted as having two modes: the normal control mode, when it operates like an ordinary controller, and a tracking mode, when the controller is tracking so that it matches given inputs and outputs.

**Reading.**
- **Windup and back-calculation are as the declaration names them.** Windup is the integrator building up while the loop
  is broken; back-calculation feeds the saturation error back to the integrator.
- **"Conditional integration" by that name is not in either fetched source.** The nearest statements are "inhibiting
  integration whenever the output saturates" (a velocity-algorithm method, which Åström calls equivalent to
  back-calculation) and "integration stops when the valve stops". ETM's frozen integrator is that method.
- **The textbook's answer to an operator taking over is tracking, not freezing (flagged).** For manual mode, Åström &
  Murray make the integrator *track* the manual input so there is "no transient when switching to automatic control"
  (bumpless transfer). S7's suspension (actuation held off, plant drifting) is closest to this manual-mode case, so a fair
  H0 for S7 includes tracking as well as back-calculation. A frozen integrator is not the tracking scheme: it resumes with
  the pre-pause integral, not one matched to the drifted plant. The declaration's forecast that freezing "is conditional
  integration" is supported only in the weaker form above.
