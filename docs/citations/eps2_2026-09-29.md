## T2. A shared resource: preemption, fair share and reservation in clusters

### Kubernetes, "Pod Priority and Preemption"

- **Page:** https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/pod-priority-preemption/ ("Last modified June 24,
  2026"; the page describes Kubernetes v1.37), fetched 2026-09-29; HTML sha256
  8d5b65c617aa5c24bab8d8d91b7170a78edfc838363d86565b161d57d71a6389.
- **Used by:** T2 (H0: what preemption does to the preempted party).

> Priority indicates the importance of a Pod relative to other Pods. If a Pod cannot be scheduled, the scheduler tries to preempt (evict) lower priority Pods to make scheduling of the pending Pod possible.

> Preemption logic tries to find a Node where removal of one or more Pods with lower priority than P would enable P to be scheduled on that Node. If such a Node is found, one or more lower priority Pods get evicted from the Node. After the Pods are gone, P can be scheduled on the Node.

> When Pod P preempts one or more Pods on Node N, nominatedNodeName field of Pod P's status is set to the name of Node N. This field helps the scheduler track resources reserved for Pod P and also gives users information about preemptions in their clusters.

> When Pods are preempted, the victims get their graceful termination period . They have that much time to finish their work and exit. If they don't, they are killed.

> An example use case is for data science workloads. A user may submit a job that they want to be prioritized above other workloads, but do not wish to discard existing work by preempting running pods.

- **Pages (Hadoop 3.5.0, "Last Published: 2026-03-24"), fetched 2026-09-29:**

- **Used by:** T2 (H0: guaranteed share, return of a share, reservation).

Capacity Scheduler:

> Capacity Guarantees - Queues are allocated a fraction of the capacity of the grid in the sense that a certain capacity of resources will be at their disposal.

> Elasticity - Free resources can be allocated to any queue beyond its capacity. When there is demand for these resources from queues running below capacity at a future point in time, as tasks scheduled on these resources complete, they will be assigned to applications on queues running below the capacity (preemption is also supported).

> The CapacityScheduler supports preemption of container from the queues whose resource usage is more than their guaranteed capacity.

Fair Scheduler:

> Fair scheduling is a method of assigning resources to applications such that all apps get, on average, an equal share of resources over time.

> minSharePreemptionTimeout : number of seconds the queue is under its minimum share before it will try to preempt containers to take resources from other queues. If not set, the queue will inherit the value from its parent queue. Default value is Long.MAX_VALUE, which means that it will not preempt containers until you set a meaningful value.

> Instantaneous Fair Share - The queue’s instantaneous fair share of resources. These shares consider only actives queues (those with running applications), and are used for scheduling decisions. Queues may be allocated resources beyond their shares when other queues aren’t using them. A queue whose resource consumption lies at or below its instantaneous fair share will never have its containers preempted.

Reservation System:

> The ReservationSystem of YARN provides the user the ability to reserve resources over (and ahead of) time, to ensure that important production jobs will be run very predictably.

> Step 7 The Scheduler will then provide containers from a special queue created to ensure resources reservation is respected. Within the limits of the reservation, the user has guaranteed access to the resources, above that resource sharing proceed with standard Capacity/Fairness sharing.

### Verma et al., "Large-scale cluster management at Google with Borg"

- **Identifier:** EuroSys'15, April 21–24, 2015, Bordeaux, France (as printed in the PDF), PDF at
  https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/43438.pdf (PDF created 2015-03-18),

- **Integrity:** PDF sha256 2fdacd3b69f8af91477412fc91d1d858a43e764929a4edb646bd517ededdad94.
- **Used by:** T2 (H0: what happens to a preempted task).

> Every job has a priority, a small positive integer. A high-priority task can obtain resources at the expense of a lower-priority one, even if that involves preempting (killing) the latter.

> If the machine selected by the scoring phase doesn’t have enough available resources to ﬁt the new task, Borg preempts (kills) lower-priority tasks, from lowest to highest priority, until it does. We add the preempted tasks to the scheduler’s pending queue, rather than migrate or hibernate them.

> Although a preempted task will often be rescheduled elsewhere in the cell, preemption cascades could occur if a high-priority task bumped out a slightly lower-priority one, which bumped out another slightly-lower priority task, and so on. To eliminate most of this, we disallow tasks in the production priority band to preempt one another.

> Tasks can ask to be notiﬁed via a Unix SIGTERM signal before they are preempted by a SIGKILL, so they have time to clean up, save state, ﬁnish any currently-executing requests, and decline new ones.

**Reading (T2).**
- **Preemption and reservation are documented practice.** Kubernetes and Borg preempt (evict or kill) lower-priority
  work. YARN's Capacity Scheduler gives each queue a guaranteed share, lends idle capacity to others, and returns it
  when the owning queue has demand again. YARN's Reservation System reserves resources ahead of time with guaranteed
  access, which is the nearest documented form of ETM-R.
- **"A preemption credit, returning the unit at resume" is not found in these words in any fetched source (flagged).**
  - In Kubernetes and Borg the preempted party gets nothing back. Victims are terminated after a grace period (Borg:
    re-queued, not migrated or hibernated). The only reservation Kubernetes names is for the *preemptor*
    (nominatedNodeName).
  - The return-at-resume mechanism that does exist is YARN's guarantee plus preemption. It returns the share when the
    idle queue has demand again, as borrowed tasks complete, or by preemption.
  - Under the Fair Scheduler this return is not immediate or on by default. Instantaneous fair share counts only active
    queues, so a paused (idle) queue has none. It reclaims its minimum share by preemption only after
    minSharePreemptionTimeout, which defaults to never.
  - So the H0 as written is a configured YARN-style guarantee, not a default of cluster schedulers. The "credit" is the
    declaration's own term.
- **Consequence for T2's Q.** The sources support the declaration's point that "the world does not wait" by default
  (Kubernetes, Borg, YARN Fair with default timeouts). They support ETM-R's zero stake only where a guarantee or
  reservation is configured. The model's ρ_back = 0.2 per step has no counterpart in the sources.
- **What the sources do not supply.** Nothing on an agent paying c per step to hold its unit; the nearest is Kubernetes'
  non-preempting class, which protects others' running work, not the holder's.

**Reading (T3).**
- **The H0 family is as the declaration names it.** Both papers down-weight a stale update by a decreasing function of
  staleness. FedAsync's own example, s = 1/(t − τ + 1), is the declaration's WALL weight 1/(1 + staleness), and FedBuff
  cites FedAsync for its scaling.
- **Staleness is counted in server updates, not in "rounds" or in time (qualifies the declaration's wording).**
  - In FedAsync, t is the global epoch, and each epoch is *one* model received from *one* worker. So t − τ counts
    server updates made by other clients' arrivals. With K = 10 clients, one declared "server round" can be up to ten
    FedAsync epochs.
  - In FedBuff, τ counts server steps (model versions), and each step aggregates a buffer of K client updates. That is
    closer to a "round".
  - In neither paper is staleness wall-clock time. It is the count of server model versions, the server's own event
    clock. So time offline costs a client influence only in proportion to how many server updates happen meanwhile.
  - The implementer should say which count WALL uses, and whether "rounds" means FedAsync epochs (one update each) or
    FedBuff steps (K updates each). The stake at L = 20 scales with that choice.
- **The exponent is not the papers' default (flagged).** The declaration's WALL weight has exponent 1 (FedAsync's
  Remark-2 example, the polynomial with a = 1). FedAsync's experiments use a = 0.5 (and a hinge with a = 10, b = 4), and
  FedBuff uses 0.5 in all but one table (10 in Table 6). At a = 0.5 the influence lost to staleness is smaller, so
  WALL/H0's stake and the client's willingness to pay b per round depend on a. The batch should run H0 at a = 0.5 as
  well, or state why not.
- **The weight is a mixing rate, not a share of an average.**
  - In FedAsync the staleness weight scales α_t in x_t = (1 − α_t)x_{t−1} + α_t x_new. It is not a client's share among
    K. It is also "Optional" (the Constant strategy has s = 1).
  - In FedBuff the scaled update is averaged over the buffer of K. The Algorithm 1 listing does not show the scaling;
    it appears in "Practical Improvements".
  - "Influence" in T3 should be defined as one of these.
- **Two inconsistencies inside FedBuff, as printed.** The appendix takes τ ≥ 1 with τ = 1 meaning "no staleness", so the
  scaling as printed gives a fresh update weight 1/√2, not 1. It also says "t − τk(t) server updates have taken place"
  for an update that started from w_{t−τk(t)}, which by the rest of the sentence should be about τk(t). Neither affects
  the declaration's H0, but an implementation copying FedBuff should fix the offset.
- **Consistent with ETM differing by construction.** Neither paper counts staleness in the client's own local steps, and
  in FedAsync the server's scheduler "controls the staleness". So ETM differs from H0 by construction, as the
  declaration says.
- **The battery trade-off (qualifies the commercial quantity).** FedAsync states that on-device learning tasks run "only
  when the devices are idle, charging, and connected to unmetered networks". The domain's practice already avoids
  spending battery to stay online. A client paying b per round to stay awake is outside that practice, which weakens
  "battery-rounds spent staying awake" as a commercial quantity.

**Reading (T4).**
- **Rollback under oversight is documented practice.** OpenAI's paper recommends that actions reviewed only after the fact
  be "more easily reversible", alongside approval for high-stakes actions and time-outs until a human reauthorizes. Leave
  no Trace treats reversibility as safety in RL, with early aborts and resets. It is about the environment's reset, not
  an overseer removing credited work. Neither source discusses the agent's incentive under rollback.
- **The fetched approval literature is not the declaration's H0 (flagged).**
  - The declaration's H0 is "reward only for committed (approved) work", an outcome reward. Christiano's approval-direction
    and MONA's non-myopic approval are instead ratings of each *action* by the overseer, optimised myopically.
  - Both sources claim this is what removes the incentive to manipulate oversight: "Arthur will make no effort to
    manipulate these judgments"; the MONA agent "does not have any reason to learn to use opaque strategies".
  - So the literature's answer to T4's suppression incentive (paying c to make steps harder to inspect) is myopic
    per-step approval. That is a third arm, neither H0 nor ETM-E. A fair T4 should include it, or say why not.
- **The suppression behaviour is a recognised failure.** MONA names subverting evaluations "by obfuscating aspects of
  their actions". That supports modelling c as a real option. The sources give no cost or rate for it.
- **The busywork result has a domain theorem behind it (flagged; qualifies the forecast PROPOSES).**
  - ETM-E's "effort credit" pays for each step taken and keeps the credit when the step is reverted. So a
    step-and-revert loop returns the agent to the same state with net positive credit.
  - That is exactly the positive-reward cycle of Ng, Harada & Russell: bicycle circles, and the soccer robot
    "touching the ball as frequently as possible".
  - Their Theorem 1 says only potential-based shaping, which sums to zero around every cycle, is guaranteed safe.
    Crediting progress (a potential) rather than effort removes the loop, which is H0's committed-work reward.
  - So the *direction* of T4's Q ("forcing zero stake onto a rollback creates a perverse incentive") is predicted by a
    known theorem. Only the busywork *share* at the declared parameters is not.
  - The declaration's statement "no domain theorem for the busywork share is expected" holds for the number. It does not
    hold for the effect, which reads as REDUNDANT-DOMAIN.
- **What the sources do not supply.** No revert probabilities, no k, no self-revert option, no busywork share.
