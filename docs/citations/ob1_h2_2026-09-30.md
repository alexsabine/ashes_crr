### h2-B1: long-horizon sequential knowledge editing (open)

**B-a** (REVIVE, arXiv:2601.11042v2):
> Sequential knowledge editing in large language models often causes catastrophic collapse of the model’s general abilities, especially for parameter-modifying methods.

**B-b** (2026, 2026, 2025):
> Despite the development of several sequential knowledge editing methods, including RECT (Gu et al., 2024), PRUNE (Ma et al., 2024), and ALPHAEDIT (Fang et al., 2024), long-horizon degradation remains largely unresolved. (REVIVE, v2 May 2026, ACL 2026)

> Under long-stream evaluations, many existing L&E editors exhaust their usable editing lifespan within only a few thousand sequential edits, making it a central bottleneck to extend the effective horizon of in-weight editing. (NAS, arXiv:2602.02543v3, May 2026)

> Knowledge editing is reliant on local weight modification and collapses quickly. Even methods designed for lifelong editing converge to pre-update performance early. So large-scale lifelong editing requires significantly improving existing methods. (WikiBigEdit, arXiv:2503.05683v2, ICML 2025)

**B-c** (REVIVE Table 1, "Performance on sequential editing over 10,000 Samples."; CounterFact then ZsRE; columns Eff., Para.,
Neigh., Flu., Consis., then ZsRE Eff., Para., Neigh.; the first row is the unedited model):
> LLaMA3 7.02 9.44 89.73 635.47 24.24 35.67 34.81 31.83

> AlphaEdit 62.48 56.9 52.31 505.5 4.25 90.57 85.66 30.5 +REVIVE 98.74 ↑58.0% 90.08 ↑58.4% 60.19 ↑15.1%

> NSE 77.59 44.42 86.12 607.86 23.31 45.61 45.04 31.27 +REVIVE 98.89 ↑27.4% 92.28 ↑107.8% 65.72 ↓23.6%

> REVIVE enhanced methods maintains an overall average 86.34% of [...] its performance across all tasks after 10, 000 edits.

*Gap (agent).* Two gaps remain after 10,000 edits:
- **Locality.** At high efficacy (Eff. above 95), the best neighbourhood success is 65.72 (NSE+REVIVE). The unedited LLaMA3
  scores 89.73, a gap of **24.01 points**.
- **General ability (GLUE).** The best editors retain **86.34%** of the unedited model, a 13.66% shortfall.

On real edits (HoReN, arXiv:2605.08143v2, Table 6, WikiBigEdit, LLaMA-3.1-8B; columns Rel., Gen., Loc.):
> 3000 AlphaEdit 0.799 0.651 0.169 HoReN 0.986 0.754 0.631

> HoReN sustains OP ≥0.93 through 50K edits, while every baseline collapses or plateaus before 10K

On the synthetic ZsRE stream, a parameter-preserving codebook editor now holds up to 50K edits. On real WikiBigEdit edits, its
generalization is 0.754 and its locality 0.631. The ideal of 1.0 is the metrics' definition; the paper does not state it as
a target.

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions:**
- **Locate-and-edit and null-space editors (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit).** They edit fixed layers and use precomputed statistics
  of the knowledge to preserve (LOKI, arXiv:2606.19679v1):
  > One unresolved challenge is that existing methods modify a fixed set of layers for all new knowledge samples, reducing flexibility and increasing catastrophic forgetting. Another is requiring access to previous knowledge and extensive pre-processing to obtain data statistics.
- **Lifelong normalization (UltraEdit, RLEdit, StableEdit; arXiv:2605.11836v2).** Running mean and variance are kept over all
  processed edits, indexed by the cumulative sample count, so the clock is the edit count, with a warm-up:
  > normalizes value gradients using running statistics

  > performing a small number of in-domain edits before the target sequence can precondition the running statistics
- **Norm anchoring (NAS).** The original model is the fixed reference:
  > rescaling each solved value vector to an original-model reference norm
- **Codebook, parameter-preserving editors (GRACE, WISE, HoReN).** They store every edit, and their memory grows linearly.
  They use a fixed per-model threshold and treat edits as independent:
  > a frozen base model with one MLP layer wrapped by a discrete key–value memory, so each edit is stored once and never re-touched

  > HoReN’s codebook grows linearly in the number of edits

  > The current single-key argmax routing treats edits as mutually independent and uses a fixed per-model threshold c
- **Spectral protection (REVIVE).** The original weights' dominant subspace is held fixed, and edits arrive in fixed rounds:
  > the dominant subspace in REVIVE is identified using a singular-value energy criterion

  > applied in 100 rounds of 100 edits each

**B-e: no.** The named benchmarks use 6–8B models (GPT-J, LLaMA3) and 10,000 sequential edits, and locate-and-edit needs
covariance statistics over a Wikipedia sample. A GPT-2-small proxy on ZsRE/CounterFact would run on CPU in hours. It would not
be the named benchmark.

### h2-B2: parametric knowledge updating under real-world drift (open)

**B-a** (arXiv:2604.05096v2):
> Large language models (LLMs) acquire most of their knowledge during pretraining, which ties them to a fixed snapshot of the world and makes adaptation to continuously evolving knowledge challenging.

**B-b** (2026; 2025):
> These results suggest that parameter-level updates struggle to preserve previously acquired knowledge while incorporating new information.

> their effectiveness remains limited when adapting to continuous knowledge drift

> Continual learning has been a longstanding challenge in AI, and persists in the era of large language models (Sparse memory finetuning, arXiv:2510.15103v1, Oct 2025)

**B-c** (arXiv:2604.05096v2, Table 2, exact-match accuracy, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct). The cells are in reading order:
Historical, C1, C2, C3, Commonsense, Overall (the mean of the five).
> Method Historical Contemporary Commonsense Overall C1 C2 C3

> Direct Generation LLaMA-3.1 45.95 11.31 1.38 1.69 45.53 21.17

> LoRA FTLLaMA-3.1 35.14 71.15 4.13 10.17 40.27 32.17

> ChronosLLaMA-3.1 50.45+3.6 92.40+12.1 63.76+16.5 61.02+28.8 50.55+2.6 63.64+24.0

*Gap (agent).* The best parametric method (LoRA fine-tuning) reaches an Overall of 32.17. The paper's time-aware retrieval
baseline scores 63.64 on the same backbone, a gap of **31.47**. LoRA fine-tuning also forgets: Historical falls from 45.95 to
35.14 (**10.81 points**). The target is a non-parametric reference, not an upper bound on parametric learning.

The second B-c source (arXiv:2510.15103v1) uses held-out NaturalQuestions; its target is no drop:
> When training on a stream of TriviaQA facts, performance on NaturalQuestions drops by 89% with full finetuning and 71% with LoRA, but only 11% with sparse memory finetuning with the same level of retention.

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions:**
- **Parametric updating (ROME, MEMIT, WISE, LoRA).** Updates are made per instance or per batch:
  > because parametric methods typically update the model either one instance at a time or in a batch-wise manner, newly acquired knowledge can overwrite both the model’s original old knowledge and earlier updates
- **Retrieval.** All evidence is stored, and every fact carries an external calendar timestamp:
  > a time-aware retrieval baseline, Chronos, which progressively organizes retrieved evidence into an Event Evolution Graph

  > (subject, relation, object, timestamp)
- **Sparse memory finetuning.** It needs a memory-layer architecture and access statistics on a background corpus:
  > the top t memory slots that are more frequently accessed on a certain batch relative to some background corpus (e.g. pretraining data)
- **Replay** is the standard (arXiv:2605.10640v1, ICML 2026):
  > While classical CPT techniques like data replay have become the standard paradigm

**B-e: no.** The backbones are an 8B model and a 1.3B memory-layer model. The benchmark data are public, but not at CPU-hours scale.

### h2-B3: continual fine-tuning of LLMs on task streams (TRACE) (open)

**B-a** (TRACE, arXiv:2310.06762v1, 2023):
> Our experiments show that after training on TRACE, aligned LLMs exhibit significant declines in both general ability and instruction-following capabilities.

**B-b** (2026):
> the primary challenge in continual LLM fine-tuning remains catastrophic forgetting, that is, training on new tasks leads to a sustained performance decline on previously learned tasks and a degradation of the pre-training LLM’s inherent general-purpose knowledge (EoupCT, arXiv:2609.30935v1, NeurIPS 2026)

> Continual supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the de facto recipe for adapting large language models (LLMs) to a stream of downstream tasks, but it suffers from catastrophic forgetting of earlier capabilities. (OPR, arXiv:2605.29495v1)

> forgetting at the LLM scale is not a scaled-up version of the classical problem but a qualitatively different one (survey arXiv:2603.12658v2)

**B-c** (Muon-OGD, arXiv:2605.08949v2, Table 2):
> Table 2: TRACE benchmark performance with the LLaMA-2-7B-Chat backbone model.

> Method LLaMA-2-7B-Chat AA (%) BT (%) SeqFT 23.0 -8.3 LoRASeqFT 9.2 -24.6 O-LoRA 41.3 -6.2 OSFT 48.4 -7.1 Ours 49.4±0.3 -4.7±1.2 PerTaskFT 57.6 N/A MTL 52.3 N/A

*Gap (agent).* The best continual method reaches an AA of 49.4. It is **2.9** below multi-task training (52.3) and **8.2**
below per-task fine-tuning (57.6), with BT −4.7 against 0. The MTL and PerTaskFT figures equal TRACE's own 2023 Table 3 (MT
w/o. Re 52.3, SingleFT 57.6), so they are carried over, not rerun.

With replay (OPR, TRACE, 7–8B):
> A 1% Vanilla Replay budget recovers most of the degradation (Wu et al., 2024) but leaves a 3–5 pp BWT gap.

> OPR-RU consistently improves over Vanilla Replay. It cuts |BWT| by 42–46% across the three backbones at ρ=0.01

OPR's MTL upper bound is shown only in its Figure 1, with no number in the text.

General knowledge (EoupCT Table 1, Qwen3-8B, best row). The cells are SuperNI ROUGE and Fgt. for three orders, then MMLU
STEM, Humanity and Other Acc. and Fgt.:
> + EOUPCT 52.9 4.4 52.1 6.0 51.5 5.7 64.7 1.4 75.9 3.0 75.5 1.4 Qwen3-4B

Task forgetting is 4.4–6.0 ROUGE, and MMLU forgetting is 1.4–3.0 points, against 0.

*Judgement.* General-knowledge forgetting under this method is small. The task-stream gap is the open part.

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions:**
- **Orthogonal-gradient and per-task LoRA methods.** They need known task boundaries, and they store per-task gradients or
  adapters. They would also need pre-training gradients, which are unknown:
  > method involves storing the gradients of previously learned tasks and projecting the training gradients of the new task onto the orthogonal subspace spanned by these stored gradients

  > orthogonal low-rank adaptation (OLoRA) [31] allocates a distinct set of LoRA parameters [7] to each new task

  > as the training data and gradients of off-the-shelf pre-training LLMs are typically unknown, employing techniques such as gradient orthogonalization is rendered unrealistic
- **Replay.** Past prompts are stored. The protocol fixes per-task epochs and shared hyperparameters in advance:
  > rolls out the most recent checkpoint on a small budget of historical prompts

  > [5, 3, 7, 5, 3, 5, 5, 7] (chosen to roughly equalize the number of gradient steps across tasks). The same hyperparameters are applied to every method and every backbone
- **The field's assumptions, as summarised by a 2026 single-author preprint** (TFGN, arXiv:2605.15053v2; its own solution
  claim is not relied on):
  > Every published continual-learning method either retains a buffer of prior data, requires task identifiers at training or inference time, applies a regularization penalty that scales poorly with model size, or operates at sentence-classification scale

**B-e: no.** The models are 7–8B. TRACE is public, but training needs GPUs.

### h2-B4: multimodal continual instruction tuning (open)

**B-a** (MCITlib, arXiv:2508.07307v3):
> the rise of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) brings new challenges in Multimodal Continual Learning (MCL), where models are expected to address both catastrophic forgetting and cross-modal coordination

**B-b** (Dec 2025; 2026):
> current multimodal continual instruction methods partially mitigate forgetting on downstream tasks but transfer poorly to general-purpose benchmarks, often degrading the model’s original capabilities. Bridging this gap is a central direction for future work on continual learning for MLLMs.

> multimodal continual learning (MM-CL) remains underexplored (survey arXiv:2603.12658v2)

**B-c** (MCITlib Table 3(a), UCIT, LLaVA-1.5):
> Method Venue ImgNet-R ArxivQA VizWiz IconQA CLEVR Flickr30k MFT (↑) MFN (↑) MAA (↑) BWT (↑)

> Individual - 91.67 90.83 57.87 78.43 76.63 61.72 76.19 - - -

> DISCO ICCV-25 87.43 93.07 46.96 68.13 65.70 56.69 75.87 69.66 81.60 -7.45

> serves as an empirical upper bound on performance, assuming no catastrophic forgetting.

*Gap (agent).* The best final accuracy is DISCO's MFN of 69.66. It is **6.53** below per-task fine-tuning (76.19) and 6.21
below its own MFT (75.87), which the paper states as the empirical upper bound.

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions** (MLLM-CTBench, arXiv:2508.08275v3; MCITlib configuration):
- **Replay.** Samples are stored or synthesized.
- **Task-conditioned architecture.** Modules are added per task.
- **Model fusion.** Task-specific checkpoints are merged after training.

> (2) Replay-based methods revisit stored or synthesized samples to reduce forgetting [40, 25, 26, 27], at cost of memory or compute. (3) Architecture-based methods add task-conditioned components (e.g., progressive expansion or module routing) [41, 28, 29], alleviating interference but potentially increasing inference overhead. (4) Model-fusion-based strategies merge task-specific checkpoints post hoc

The UCIT benchmark has 6 tasks, and the expert count is fixed at that number in advance:
> rank = 96, expert num = 6

**B-e: no.** The backbones are LLaVA-1.5 (7B) and InternVL.

### h2-B5: long-horizon task identification for expert routing (open)

**B-a** (FLEX, arXiv:2608.01437v1):
> Many recent methods maintain task-specific LoRA experts and route each input to one or more experts at inference. Yet the task-identification problem underlying expert routing remains under-explored.

**B-b** (Aug 2026):
> Therefore, the transferred CIL routers remove a substantial fraction of task-identification error, but the remaining gaps leave meaningful room for stronger routers.

> Textual fingerprints that leak task identity and short 4–10-task sequences with few competing experts jointly obscure the long-horizon routing problem.

**B-c** (FLEX Table 3, MacroScore on the 34-task benchmark):
> Method Base Best CIL Oracle Gap Recovered (%) DISCO 50.32 54.93 (+4.61) 60.01 5.08 47.6

> PureLoRA 54.60 56.14 (+1.54) 60.04 3.90 28.3

> Oracle is a routing reference rather than a guaranteed global upper bound

*Gap (agent).* The best absolute score is 56.14, against ground-truth routing at 60.04, a gap of **3.90**. For DISCO the gap
is 5.08. The best routers recover 28–50% of the base-to-oracle gap.

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions:**
- **Per-task experts.** One expert is created per task, at a task boundary supplied from outside.
- **Routing.** A classifier is trained on the ground-truth task label:
  > each task defines an incremental routing class

  > We denote ground-truth one-hot routing as Oracle.
- **Agent setting** (ACLArena). The environment reveals the task:
  > MLE routes to the corresponding expert using the interaction environment available in the agent execution context, without requiring benchmark identities or ground-truth task labels.

**B-e: no.** The experts are MLLM experts. The router sub-problem could be proxied on frozen features on CPU, but that would
not be the benchmark.

### h2-B6: multi-stage post-training consolidation (agent continual learning) (open)

**B-a** (ACLArena, arXiv:2609.23989v1):
> Building general-purpose agents for industrial deployment requires integrating multiple capabilities, each typically acquired at a distinct stage of training.

**B-b** (Sep 2026; 2026 survey):
> Yet there is currently no well-established recipe for Agent Continual Learning (ACL), with little understanding of the trade-offs among existing integration paradigms.

> these results suggest that the problem cannot be solved simply by finding a better shared checkpoint

> meaning gains at one stage can be silently erased by the next—a structural challenge with no adequate solution yet. (survey arXiv:2603.12658v2, on CPT → CFT → alignment)

**B-c** (ACLArena Table 2, Qwen3-8B-Base). The first four cells are in-domain: AIME26 (math), NQ (search), τ3-Retail
(e-commerce) and IF-Eval.
> ExpertMath 25.83 22.0±0.2 2.3±0.5 43.3±0.6

> ExpertSearch 9.79 49.9±1.7

> ExpertE-commerce 6.25 19.3±0.1 33.2±1.1

> ExpertIF 0.00 26.4±1.1 4.6±0.2 86.2±0.0

> Seq-Final 10.21 33.5±1.7 29.6±1.6 84.8±0.1

> +MMOPD 21.25 45.2±0.4 27.7±1.5 84.6±0.5

> MLE (Ours) 21.04 49.7±0.2 32.9±2.2 85.0±0.3

*Gap (agent).*
- **The best single shared checkpoint (+MMOPD)** reaches 21.25, 45.2, 27.7 and 84.6. The per-task oracles reach 25.83, 49.9,
  33.2 and 86.2. The gaps are **4.58, 4.7, 5.5 and 1.6**.
- **The parameter-isolated routed experts (MLE)** close every gap except math (4.79).

**B-d, the method families and their assumptions:**
- **Merging** uses fixed uniform weights.
- **Distillation** learns from per-stage oracles.
- **Isolated experts** add one expert per stage.

In all three, the stages are supplied from outside, fixed in number and order:
> MM uniformly averages the parameters.

> these residuals are parameter-isolated: learning a new stage introduces a new lightweight LoRA expert

> Our experiments mainly focus on Qwen3-8B-Base and a fixed four-stage curriculum.

**B-e: no.** The model is 8B, and the pipeline includes RL stages.

### Not shown open

**h2-B7: CPT forgetting with replay available.** B-a and B-c were found; no B-b. The continual method *exceeds* the
joint-training target, and no 2025–26 source was found stating that this setting is open (arXiv:2508.01908v1, Aug 2025).
> the introduction of new data often causes distribution shifts, leading to performance degradation on previously learned tasks

> The joint training baseline average is only 72.6, while 25% replay with Reptile yields 76.8, and 50% replay with Reptile achieves the best overall result of 77.1.

The assumptions are the replay of earlier data and three supplied phases:
> Joint Training Baseline: Model trained on the joint A+B+C dataset in an i.i.d. setting together from scratch.

> Our study is largely limited to three tasks in a fixed sequence.

**h2-B8: replay-free, task-free continual pre-training at LLM scale.** B-a and B-b were found; no B-c. The only 2026 source
(TFGN, arXiv:2605.15053v2) is a single-author preprint that also claims the problem solved (BWT −0.007). It reports no joint
or replay reference.
> Continually pre-training a large language model on a sequence of heterogeneous text domains, without replay and without task labels, has remained an unsolved architectural problem at LLM scale.

> unsolved on a ∼9 B transformer under the replay-free, task-free regime that production deployment requires

**h2-B9: forgetting in continual RL post-training.** B-a and B-b were found. The numbers exist but have **no stated target**,
so they are not tagged B-c. The paper's own method is 1.68 below its own MFT, and it is above the pretrained model on external
benchmarks (MRCL/CPO, arXiv:2607.04364v3):
> Experiments on MRCL show that standard reinforcement learning still suffers from catastrophic forgetting during continual post-training.

> GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) 65.03 30.70 45.17 83.00 44.80 74.26 53.74 63.94

> CPO (Ours) 90.83 76.84 63.64 77.50 49.00 73.24 71.56 72.81

> CPO achieves an average score of 64.14%, compared with 49.55% for GSPO and 59.50% for the pretrained model.

> catastrophic forgetting in multi-modal reasoning remains underexplored, and the role of policy regularization in continual RL is still not well understood.

**h2-B10: stability gap in continual pre-training.** B-a (2024) and B-b (2026 survey) were found. No 2025–26 number against a
target was found.
> we observed a temporary performance drop at the beginning, followed by a recovery phase, a phenomenon known as the "stability gap,"

> A key open direction is understanding why the “stability gap” occurs

## Judgement calls (for the investigator's review)

1. **h2-B1's targets are the unedited model.** Its locality (Neigh. 89.73) and its general ability (100%) are the targets.
   Neither is a "joint training" number, but both are the natural oracle for editing. The WikiBigEdit ideal of 1.0 is the
   metrics' definition, not a number the source states.
2. **h2-B2's target is a retrieval baseline on the same backbone** (63.64). It is a non-parametric reference, not an upper
   bound on parametric learning. The second B-c (the NQ drop) has "no drop" as its target.
3. **h2-B3 is open on the task-stream gap.** It is 2.9 points to MTL and 8.2 to per-task fine-tuning; both references were
   carried over from TRACE 2023. General-knowledge forgetting under EoupCT is small (1.4–3.0 MMLU points). If only the
   2.9-point MTL gap counts, h2-B3 is the thinnest of the six open bottlenecks.
4. **h2-B5 and h2-B6 overlap in mechanism.** Both are about per-task experts and routing. They are kept apart because their
   benchmarks and targets differ: an oracle router, versus oracle per-task experts.
5. **h2-B9 is not shown open.** It has a 2026 open quote and numbers, but the source states no target. The paper's own method
   sits 1.68 below its MFT and above the pretrained model. It could be promoted if MFT is accepted as a target, as MCITlib
   states it.
6. **h2-B7 is recorded as not shown open because the target was met,** not because it was dropped.
7. **Every open bottleneck in H2 is CPU-infeasible at benchmark scale.** Small proxies (GPT-2-small editing on
   ZsRE/CounterFact; routing on frozen features) are possible, but they are not the named benchmarks.
