# ROB1 citation checks, family R1: programmes (ARIA first; DARPA, EU Horizon/euROBIN and NSF as corroboration) (2026-09-30; R10)

This is stage 1 of `Robotics/DECLARATION.md` (pushed at b61e0a9), family **R1**: the robotics programmes and opportunity
spaces of ARIA (the UK Advanced Research and Invention Agency), the targets they state, and the reasons they give that the
targets are not met today, with corroboration from other public programmes that state robotics targets. The dossier records
bottlenecks only. It makes no CRR reading; that is stage 2. The double check (stage 1b) is done by a different agent.

**How the sources were handled.** Every source was fetched on the day (2026-09-30) through the session proxy, with `curl`
and TLS verification on:
- **ARIA:** the opportunity-space index, every page of the Adaptive Machines opportunity space (Robot Dexterity with its
  funded-projects and funding pages, Enduring Atmospheric Platforms with its funded-projects and funding pages, Robot
  Locomotion, Ubiquitous Particle Profiling, opportunity seeds), the Safeguarded AI and Universal Fabricators pages, and the
  PDFs they link (programme theses, calls for proposals, the TA3 position paper).
- **DARPA:** programme and challenge pages and news releases on darpa.mil (RACER, Lift Challenge, SubT Challenge, Triage
  Challenge).
- **arXiv:** the abstract page (current version, version history, comments) and the PDF of the current version.
- **Other:** the European Commission's Horizon Europe Work Programme 2026-2027 PDF, the NSF FRR programme page, the Frontiers
  full-text HTML, an author-hosted accepted manuscript, an institutional-repository PDF, a GitHub results README fetched raw,
  a company press release and one trade-press article.

Text was extracted as follows:
- **PDFs:** pymupdf 1.28.2 (`page.get_text()`).
- **HTML:** BeautifulSoup 4 `get_text('\n')` after removing script, style, noscript and svg; runs of spaces and blank lines
  were collapsed.
- **The Robothon README (Markdown):** used as fetched.

Quotes are copied from that text. Only whitespace is normalised (and hyphens at line breaks rejoined by the verifier). Table
rows are quoted as the extractor emitted them, cell by cell in reading order. Fetched files are held outside the repository,
under `/tmp/claude-0/rob_src/r1/`:
- `src/`: the fetched files;
- `txt/`: the extracted text;
- `search/queries.txt`: the search log.

Their sha256 is recorded below and in `/tmp/claude-0/rob_src/r1/SHA256SUMS.txt` (108 files).

**Rules for the numbers.**
- Every number below is the source's own, on its own protocol.
- Numbers from different sources are not comparable. Where a target and a best result come from different documents, this

## Bottlenecks

The open test: B-a, B-b and B-c must all be present (the stage-1b table computes it from the roles). Five are read as open.
Two are listed as **not shown open**. Two of the five open ones carry an explicit note that the double check may find them

### r1-B1. Dexterous manipulation: speed and generality against a human (open)

- **B-a.**
  - ARIA Robot Dexterity thesis v2 (2024; r1:1): "Despite steady progress, general dexterous manipulation remains an unsolved
    problem in robotics." The current ARIA programme page (r1:2) says robots "cannot achieve the flexibility, speed, and
    precision of human manipulation".
  - The EU Horizon Europe WP 2026-2027 (r1:7) names the same capability as an expected outcome: "flexible and meticulous
    manipulation of robots in unstructured environment, with high autonomy".
- **B-b.**
  - ARIA TA3 position paper, foreword (2025; r1:3): "the gap between what machine intelligence can achieve in silico and what
    robots can physically do is becoming increasingly apparent."
  - euROBIN Coopetition report (arXiv v1, 29 Mar 2026; r1:4): "results across the three leagues confirm that autonomy in
    previously unknown, dynamic, and unstructured settings remains an open challenge, particularly for manipulation tasks"
    and "Fully autonomous manipulation remained rare".
- **B-c.** The benchmark is the Robothon electronic task-board trial protocol, judged remotely by DR.J (So et al., IEEE RAM,
  CC BY accepted version; r1:5). ARIA itself sets no numeric dexterity target, so the target is the human baseline measured on
  the same board in the same paper. The rows:
  > SHumanBody,Human* 0.6 1.6 1.4 2.4 1.7 0.4 8.1
  > SEpson-VT6,Team J 11 7 6 12 10 6 52

  "We found that the best robot performance is only 16% as fast as the best human performance across the entire set of
  tasks."

  | Actor | Full trial (s) |
  |---|---|
  | best human (N = 10; mean 18.9 s) | 8.1 |
  | best robot (Team J; also the any-robot composite) | 52 |

  The robot runs at 8.1/52 = 0.156 of the best human's speed, which is 6.4× slower (2.75× slower than the human mean). The
  robot is ahead on one subtask only: ST5, 0.2 s against 1.7 s, with a purpose-built electromagnetic fixture.

  The 2025 successor board (r1:6, Robothon README): "8 Applications, 8 Selected Teams, 1 Finisher, Best Time 106 seconds",
  under a "10-minute time limit for fully autonomous solution". There is no human time for that board, so no gap is
  computed; the completion rate is 1 of 8.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **ARIA's "Genesis paradigm"** (r1:8): rigid hardware is designed first, then "animated either by human tele-operators or
    by algorithms". Control runs on "the high frequencies and low latencies (1ms) typical of robotic control". Biology instead
    tolerates "long, highly variable sensorimotor latencies (25ms … 200ms …)". This is a **fast fixed control clock**, with
    the body fixed before the control.
  - **Learned control** (RL, multimodal LLM scene understanding; r1:8) is expected to add generality. This is a
    data/tuning assumption.
  - **Specialised fixtures** (r1:9): "a purpose-built solution excelling in a single task". This assumes the task is known in
    advance.
  - **Rehearsed or teleoperated execution** (r1:10): "prior access to the task board may have encouraged tailored,
    task-specific solutions", and "Teams could opt for teleoperation". A human is the fallback when the robot fails, which is
    an interruption assumption.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. The benchmark is a physical board timed on real robots against a human. A simulated
  manipulation proxy would not be the benchmark.

### r1-B2. Legged locomotion: range, endurance and robustness outside prepared ground (open)

- **B-a** (ARIA Robot Locomotion thesis v1.0, June 2026; r1:11): "a body that can briefly coordinate huge forces for a
  backflip is not necessarily one that can spend a shift carrying loads over uneven terrain without overheating, falling or
  breaking."
- **B-b.**
  - Jenny Read, ARIA insights, 15 Apr 2026 (r1:12): "Animals still outperform robots on the combination of agility, range, and
    robustness that real-world locomotion demands", and "robots will remain confined to prepared environments, near charging
    points, and with short operating windows."
  - The ARIA Robot Locomotion page (2026; r1:13): "Legged robots … cannot yet operate reliably for long periods beyond
    controlled environments".
- **B-c.** There are two metrics, from two independent reviews, each within its own paper.
  - **Range** (Burden et al., Science Robotics 2024, accepted version; r1:14): "The farthest walk by a legged robot on a single
    battery charge was Ranger's 65 kilometer trek over the course of 31 hours". The human baseline is that "exceptional
    athletes can run hundreds of kilometers over multiple days … over rough terrain whereas Ranger exploits the smoothness of
    the track". Batteries hold "about 50-fold less useful energy per unit mass as animal fat". Range is at least 1.5× short
    even taking "hundreds" as 100 km, and the robot figure is for smooth ground only. The source gives no single human
    number.
  - **Cost of transport** (Riener et al., Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2023; r1:15): "The COT of MIT cheetah (0.52) came
    closest of all robots to the COT of a human (0.2–0.47)". The best robot is 1.1× to 2.6× the human value. The CoT samples
    come from different references, as the authors warn.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Stiff actuation with software compliance and disturbance rejection** (r1:16): robots "imitate compliance in software
    instead of benefiting from it mechanically … treating every disturbance as an error to suppress". Every deviation from the
    reference is treated as an error.
  - **Missions cut by recharging** (r1:12): "near charging points, and with short operating windows". This is an interruption
    assumption.
  - **Feedback control for recovery** (r1:17).
  - **The comparison itself runs on a body clock** (Burden, r1:17): "we normalize time by the natural period of a runner's
    limb". On that clock, robots' "period-specific latency" is at least 1,000× better than nerves, yet robots still lose. The
    authors read the gap as one of integration, not of components.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable on the named metrics, which need physical robots. A simulated legged proxy is CPU-feasible for
  short gaits only.

### r1-B3. Persistent stratospheric platforms at low hourly cost and high recovery (open)

- **B-a** (ARIA Enduring Atmospheric Platforms thesis v2.0, late 2025; r1:18): "Existing HAPS approaches … have so far proven
  too limited, fragile, costly, or impractical to deliver a scalable solution", with "1 in 4 missions ending with aircraft
  loss".
- **B-b.**
  - ARIA call for proposals V.2, 11 Dec 2025 (r1:19): "existing HAPS approaches remain too limited to deliver a commercially
    scalable solution", and "The required power threshold of 300 W … lies significantly beyond the capability of
    state-of-the-art platforms".
  - Aerospace America (AIAA), 6 May 2025 (r1:20). This is trade press, used beside the company release: "The company has yet
    to achieve its target of 200 days of continuous flight" (the journalist's paraphrase of AALTO). The 2025 record aircraft
    "had to be ditched into the Indian Ocean"; it "had just completed its 67th day aloft in the stratosphere". The 2022
    aircraft "crashed after 64 days".
- **B-c.** The metrics are ARIA's cost and recovery metrics (thesis cost table, r1:21; call Section 3, r1:23). The target
  document and the state-of-the-art estimate are the same programme's.

  | Metric | State of the art (ARIA estimate) | Target | Gap |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | gross hourly cost | solar HALE (Zephyr) ~£7,000/hr | threshold ~£500/hr; goal ~£100/hr | 14× over the threshold; 70× over the goal |
  | loss rate | 1 in 4 lost on ascent/descent | 1 in 20 (Rredeployment > 0.95) | recovery 0.75 against > 0.95 |
  | payload power | "significantly beyond" SOTA (no watt figure quoted) | 300 W for one week (50.4 kWh) | not computable |
  | endurance | 67 days (Amprius/AALTO, April 2025; r1:22) | TWR > 1 week | **target exceeded** (in the tropics) |

  Fairness: endurance is not the open part. The 67-day flight exceeds ARIA's endurance target, though it flew from Kenya,
  not year-round near the UK. What stays open is the cost per hour, the recovery rate (both record flights ended with the
  aircraft lost) and the payload power.

  The call has an internal inconsistency: it gives the future 3 kW-for-a-week target as both "0.5 MWh" and "840 kWh", while
  3 kW × 168 h = 504 kWh. The "840 kWh" figure is not used.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **The three HAPS families** (r1:24):
    - solar HAPS: "limited by endurance (especially at high latitudes) and cost";
    - balloons: "controllability (station keeping, recovery)";
    - airships: "cost … and airspeed".
  - **Solar HAPS runs on a diurnal clock** (r1:25): "deep daily discharge cycles that shorten battery life, capping mission
    length". Battery wear is counted in days.
  - **Cost is amortised per mission hour**, with each mission a boundary carrying a probability of loss. This is the
    agent's reading of the cost metrics.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. The benchmark is a week-long flight. ARIA's techno-economic cost model could be computed on CPU,
  but it is a model, not the benchmark.

### r1-B4. Heavy-lift drones: payload-to-weight ratio (open, narrowly; likely CONTESTED)

- **B-a.**
  - DARPA Lift Challenge page (r1:26): "Current multirotor drones … payload-to-weight ratio is low, typically 1:1 or less".
    The target is "payloads more than four times their weight".
  - ARIA's locomotion thesis names the same limit (r1:27): "carrying meaningful payloads over useful distances quickly creates
    short range, noise, downwash and safety risks."
- **B-b.**
  - ARIA insights, 15 Apr 2026 (r1:28): "Delivery drones are now real, but current commercial systems remain tightly
    constrained on payload, range, wind tolerance, and noise."
  - DARPA, 11 Aug 2026 (r1:29): "While this did not achieve the Challenge goal of a 4:1 ratio, it still represents a success
    by DARPA's definition."
- **B-c** (DARPA Lift Challenge results, 11 Aug 2026; r1:30, r1:31). The benchmark is a scored 5-nautical-mile circuit, with
  aircraft of 55 lb or less.
  - Best scored: "an unprecedented payload-to-weight ratio of 3.84:1" (AVIDrone), against the goal of 4:1. That is 96% of
    the goal, 0.16 short.
  - Not scored: "it achieved the highest ratio in the competition at 9.63 to 1".
  - DARPA's own reading is that "The 4:1 payload-to-weight ratio is possible and likely just the beginning". A second
    challenge (DLC-2) doubles the course to 10 nautical miles in 2028.
  - This is open only under the programme's own scoring. The stage-1b agent should expect CONTESTED.
- **B-d** (r1:32). The families are:
  - an electric single-rotor helicopter;
  - a convertible tri-rotor;
  - turbine-exhaust-driven rotors;
  - a string-braced multirotor.

  All are airframe and propulsion designs. **No assumption about clocks, interruptions or memory is stated.** The metric is
  static: one loaded circuit.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable (flight hardware).

### r1-B5. Autonomous search in unknown, communication-denied environments (open; the benchmark result is from 2021)

- **B-a.**
  - DARPA SubT (r1:33, r1:34): the challenge seeks to "explore uncharted underground
    environments that are too dangerous, dark, or deep to risk human lives". The programme manager cites "severe
    communication constraints".
  - ARIA's locomotion thesis gives an illustrative target profile (r1:35): operation through "intermittent GPS, radio and
    visual signal loss"; "Complete 80% of route distance without teleoperation" (threshold) or 95% (stretch); and "Fewer than
    1 physical intervention per kilometre". ARIA marks it as illustrative, so it is not used as the B-c target.
- **B-b.**
  - Wang et al., survey, arXiv v3, 21 May 2025 (r1:36): "four core challenges: denied GNSS signals, large-scale scenarios,
    tough terrains, and intermittent communication", and "range constraints … pose the risk of partial system disconnections.
    This challenge becomes particularly severe in extreme environments such as subterranean voids". Learning-based planning
    is "still in its early stages, with limited real-world applications".
  - euROBIN 2026 (r1:37): "Dynamic navigation on staircases or crowded areas remained a major barrier."
- **B-c.** The benchmark is the SubT Final Event prize round (CERBERUS arXiv v1, r1:38): "every team had 60 min to find as
  many of the 40 artifacts … as possible", with a point per correct class and position within 5 m. The DARPA final scores
  (r1:39):
  > 23: CERBERUS (…), DARPA-funded winner of the $2,000,000 first place prize
  > 23: CSIRO Data61, DARPA-funded winner of the $1,000,000 second place prize

  The best is 23 of 40 (57.5%), with 17 artifacts unfound in 60 minutes. **Stale:** the result is from September 2021, and
  the benchmark has not been re-run. The 2025–26 sources above still call the problem open, but no newer number exists on
  this benchmark.
- **B-d.** The winning system's lost-link handling (CERBERUS, r1:40; the 2025 survey confirms it, r1:41):
  - **Out-of-range exploration is bounded by a clock.** "the Homing Timer block checks whether the given budget has elapsed",
    or homing is "based on remaining mission time".
  - **The link is declared lost by a count threshold, then recovered by backtracking.** "If the connection is lost (based on
    a threshold on consecutive, failed pings), it commands the robot to backtrack its path until the connection is
    reestablished".
  - **Recovery is triggered after a fixed time without motion.** "“Recovery Homing,” in case the robot does not move for a
    predefined amount of time".
  - **Store-and-forward memory.** Artifact reports are "stored on the robot and … transferred to the Base Station once the
    communication was re-established".
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable on the benchmark, a physical course; the SubT Virtual track used a full robotics simulator. The
  lost-link rules themselves could be simulated in a CPU gridworld, as a proxy.

### r1-B6. Off-road ground autonomy at human-driver speed (not shown open)

- **B-a** (DARPA RACER page; r1:42): off-road autonomy "has lagged due to the challenging complexity of
  off-road terrain environments". The target is "software performance to move off-road at speeds on par with a human driver".
- **B-b.** None found. DARPA's 14 Jan 2026 release (r1:45) reports the programme complete: the stack can be applied "to any
  vehicle … independent of GPS or pre-mapped routes, and at mission-relevant speeds".
- **B-c** (r1:43, r1:44). The DARPA numbers:

  | Experiment | Result |
  |---|---|
  | E3, 2023 | "246 miles over 24.6 total hours" (10.0 mph average); "speeds of about 25 miles per hour" |
  | E4, 2023 | "speeds up to 30 miles per hour" |

  No human-driver speed is published in the fetched pages, so no gap can be computed. The Phase 2 target, "twice that of the
  first phase performance metrics", is relative.
- **B-d** (r1:45): a platform-agnostic learned stack. "what would normally take weeks to retrain a new model can now be done
  in a day" (a tuning assumption: per-environment retraining).
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable (full-size vehicles).
- **Why it is listed:** the programme-owner's completion claim is itself contrary evidence. It is recorded rather than
  dropped.

### r1-B7. Modularity and interoperability of robot systems (not shown open)

- **B-a** (ARIA TA3 position paper, 2025; r1:46): "Most current RAS systems are not modular or easily interoperable", and
  "integration costs are embedded as a significant part of total installation cost."
- **B-b.**
  - The same paper, foreword (r1:47): "A key reason why robotics has not yet progressed as rapidly as AI is … a fragmented
    ecosystem."
  - euROBIN 2026 (r1:48): "low-level and hardware-dependent modules remained difficult to integrate due to platform
    specificity."
- **B-c.** None. No metric with a target is published. The euROBIN royalty scores are in figures only.
- **B-d** (r1:49): shared modules and a marketplace, common data formats and protocols, standards.
- **B-e.** Not a benchmark.

### Harvester errors found (quotes all verbatim; these are numbers or readings)

1. **r1-B1, best robot time.** The harvester used DR.J Table 6's 52 s (6.4× the best human's 8.1 s).
   - The organiser's own README gives "Best Time 31 seconds" for 2022. The harvester quoted this README for 2023 and 2025,
     but not for 2022.
   - The 31 s run is RoboTechX MDX, with all six tasks ticked on the image-only 2022 scorecard. It was on the same 2021
     six-task protocol.
   - On 31 s the robot is 3.8× slower than the best human, not 6.4×.
   - The two records from the same organiser disagree.
2. **r1-B1, the composite.** The AnyRobot row's per-subtask bests sum to 36.0 s, although its trial column reads 52 s. This is
   minor.
3. **r1-B2, cost of transport.** The harvester's "best robot CoT 0.52 (MIT Cheetah)" and its gap of "1.1x–2.6x" rest on
   Riener et al.'s sample of robots.
   - Ranger, which the harvester cites for range, had a CoT of 0.28 in 2011 (Cornell Chronicle). That is inside the human
     range the harvester used as its target.
   - RAIBO2 reports 0.25 (Nature 2026).
4. **r1-B2, the range floor.** "Hundreds of kilometers" implies at least 200 km, not 100 km. The range gap is therefore at
   least 3.1×, not 1.5×.
5. **r1-B3, endurance.** ARIA's TWR is endurance *within range*, that is, station-keeping near a fixed point. The Amprius
   release states 67 days aloft, not 67 days within range. "Endurance target exceeded" is therefore plausible but not shown.
6. **r1-B4, an omission.** The DARPA page the harvester quoted also doubles DLC-2's minimum payload to 220 lb. The harvester
   noted only the longer course.
7. **r1-B5, the target.** DARPA set no target score ("Teams earn points by correctly identifying artifacts"). 40/40 is the
   course ceiling, an oracle, not a programme goal.
8. **r1-B5, autonomy.** The tied 23-point run was not fully autonomous: CSIRO's human supervisor "relied heavily on
   teleoperation" at the end. Its robots also detected 27 of 40 artifacts, four of which were not reported.
