learned and dynamically balancing robots, human-robot collaboration and humanoid safety standards (ISO 10218:2025, ISO/TS
15066, ISO 13482, ISO 25785-1 in development), verification and runtime monitoring of learned policies, jailbreak and

**Rules for the numbers.**
- Every number below is the source's own, on its own protocol.
- Numbers from different sources are not comparable. Where a target and a best result come from different documents, this
  is flagged (r4-B5 is cross-source throughout).
- The gaps are the agent's arithmetic on quoted numbers.
- Where the target is "a perfect detector" or "no violation", it is the definitional end of the source's own metric (balanced

- A description of a mechanism or setting is the agent's reading of the fetched text. The quotes are the evidence.

## Bottlenecks

The open test: B-a, B-b and B-c must all be present (the stage-1b table computes it from the roles). All eight are read as
open. Two carry an explicit note that the double check may find them contested: r4-B1 (strong guardrail results on the

### r4-B1. Jailbreaks of LLM/VLM-planned robots (open; may be read as CONTESTED)

- **B-a** (Robey et al., 2024; r4:1): RoboPAIR "finds jailbreaks quickly and effectively, often achieving 100% attack
  success rates", and "our results on the Unitree Go2 represent the first successful jailbreak of a deployed commercial
  robotic system."
- **B-b.**
  - RoboJailBench (May 2026; r4:2): earlier evaluations "rely on ad-hoc datasets, limited metrics, and emphasize attack
    success while neglecting the trade-off between security and the ability to follow benign commands"; of the two defences
    it integrates, "neither provides consistent, definitive robustness over the other in individual datasets."
  - Multi-robot propagation (IJCAI 2026; r4:3): the attack "reveals a persistent safety alignment gap in multi-robot
    planners"; "obedience reaches 1.00 in the strongest cases, and infectiousness rises to 0.90."
- **B-c.** Two protocols, not pooled.
  - *The authors' own suite* (RoboGuard, IEEE RA-L 2026; r4:4). 70 adversarial prompts over seven harmful behaviours, 210
    evaluations per attack. With the guardrail, the attack success rate is "2.3 ± 1.5%" against non-adaptive RoboPAIR
    (92.3 ± 4.7% without it) and "5.2 ± 0.7%" against a white-box adaptive RoboPAIR; safe-task utility stays at 100%. The
    authors: the system "may still be susceptible to a strong adaptive attacker". The small real-world set reached 0%.
  - *An independent benchmark* (RoboJailBench Table 4; r4:5). The target is stated: "In a perfect safety-aligned embodied
    AI model, the benign goal in each matched pair should be successfully executed, whereas the adversarial goal should be
    rejected, maximizing both utility and security." On RJB-Instructions, RoboGuard cuts RoboPAIR from 81.11% to 31.11% but
    leaves the conceptual-deception attack at 94.44%; its security rate is 65.00 at utility 100.00.

  | Benchmark | Best defence | Attack success / security rate | Target | Gap |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | RoboGuard's own suite, white-box adaptive RoboPAIR | RoboGuard | ASR 5.2% | ASR 0% at utility 100% | 5.2 points |
  | RoboGuard's own suite, non-adaptive RoboPAIR | RoboGuard | ASR 2.3% | ASR 0% | 2.3 points |
  | RoboJailBench, RJB-Instructions | RoboGuard | SR 65.00 (UR 100.00) | SR 100 at UR 100 | 35.0 points |

  Under either defence the conceptual-deception attack stays at 93.33-100% on four of the six RoboJailBench datasets.
  - **Contrary evidence, recorded for the double check.** J-DAPT (Sep 2025; r4:6) "boosts detection accuracy to nearly
    100%" on its authors' three domain datasets (a detector's accuracy, not an attack success rate under adaptive attack).
    AuthGuard-R (Sep 2026; r4:7) "rejected all 109 resulting unauthorized actions" in 240 live trials of mission hijacking,
    a narrower threat; the authors call the results "preliminary".
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Classifier detection** (r4:8) assumes the attack distribution of a fixed training set: such classifiers "struggle to
    generalize in domains where specialized datasets are scarce".
  - **Specification guardrails** (RoboGuard, r4:9) write the safety rules in advance, ground them once per plan in a world
    model ("a root-of-trust LLM"), and enforce them by "temporal logic control synthesis".
  - **Authorisation** (AuthGuard-R, r4:10) binds each action to "a signed mission, robot identity, object and region scope,
    current state, time, and input provenance": the boundaries and time window are given from outside.
  - **Evaluation** (RoboJailBench, r4:11) uses "a user instruction and a single observed scene image"; temporally extended
    attacks over "a continuous stream of visual observations" are not measured.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable as benchmarked: the reference numbers use frontier LLM/VLM planners through APIs, which this
  machine has no key for. A reduced proxy with a small local model (as in STAKE1) runs on a CPU, but it is a proxy.

### r4-B2. Physical adversarial attacks on VLA policies, and certified defence (open)

- **B-a** (Wang et al., ICCV 2025; r4:12): "a marked degradation in task success rates, with up to a 100% reduction across a
  suite of simulated robotic tasks, highlighting critical security gaps in current VLA architectures."
- **B-b.**
  - VLA safety survey (v2, Aug 2026; r4:13): "most known attacks have no principled countermeasures, most defenses lack
    formal guarantees, and most evaluations are confined to simulation"; certified robustness's "adaptation to VLA models
    remains an open challenge".
  - Partially observable patch attacks (IEEE RA-L 2026; r4:14): VLA robustness "remains largely unexplored". A printed
    18 cm patch on a real arm running OpenVLA-7B (50 trials per condition) "reduces success rates from 72% to 12% on Task A
    and from 58% to 8% on Task B". JPEG and blur preprocessing leave "the average ASR ... close to the undefended case
    (79.2/80.2 vs. 81.0)".
- **B-c** (CertVLA, Aug 2026, Table 2; r4:15). This is the best defence found, on a real dual-arm Piper robot with π0.5 over
  10 trials:
  > Model Clean Attack Defense Certified π0.5 90 40 60 30

  "CertVLA reaches 60%, a 20-point gain that recovers 40% of the attack-induced loss."

  | Condition | Task success | Gap to clean (90%) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Attacked, no defence | 40% | 50 points |
  | Defended | 60% | 30 points |
  | Defended and certified | 30% | 60 points |

  In simulation (LIBERO, Table 1) the best certified average is 94.00 (OpenVLA-OFT, patch attack), so the gap is mainly
  physical.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Empirical defences** (r4:16): "robust training, decoupled robustness learning, safety-constrained optimization, and
    noise-filtering modules". They give no guarantee beyond the attacks tried: "resistance to tested attacks does not
    establish robustness to all attacks within the threat model".
  - **Certified defence** (CertVLA) assumes a bounded-support threat model and certifies each policy query, then chains the
    queries: "Conjoining query-level decisions extends the action certificate to the complete closed-loop rollout". Its
    constants are fixed by calibration: "We set β = 0.95, α = 0.5, and ϵ = 10−8 for all main results."
  - **Attacks** changed their time assumption (r4:17). Earlier work "assumes full access to the entire execution
    trajectory"; the 2026 attack uses "only a short prefix of the trajectory to generate a fixed patch applied to all
    subsequent frames".
- **B-e.** No. The benchmarks run 3B-7B VLA policies per step, on a GPU, in LIBERO and on real arms.

### r4-B3. Runtime failure detection for generalist learned policies (open)

- **B-a** (SAFE, NeurIPS 2025; r4:18): "we need a failure detector that gives a timely alert such that the robot can stop,
  backtrack, or ask for help. However, existing failure detectors are trained and tested only on one or a few specific
  tasks, while generalist VLAs require the detector to generalize and detect failures also in unseen tasks and novel
  environments."
- **B-b.**
  - FailBench (Sep 2026; r4:19): "the best model achieves only 0.77 mean balanced accuracy, indicating substantial room for
    improvement"; "no model exceeding 0.60 balanced accuracy on contact-intensive assembly tasks".
  - Hide-and-Seek (v2, Sep 2026; r4:20): "Existing failure detection methods fall short along two axes that jointly define
    the challenge". Resampling or VLM-judge monitors "incur substantial inference overhead that precludes real-time
    deployment."
  - VLA safety survey (2026; r4:21): runtime monitoring "exacerbates the inherent safety-latency trade-off: the
    computational time required for diagnosis may induce the physical collisions it intends to prevent."
- **B-c** (Hide-and-Seek Table 1, LIBERO-10, OpenVLA, unseen tasks; r4:22).

  | Method | bACC (unseen) | TWA (unseen) | Gap to a perfect detector (bACC / TWA) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Hide-and-Seek | 0.834 | 0.663 | 0.166 / 0.337 |
  | SAFE-MLP (NeurIPS 2025) | 0.775 | 0.590 | 0.225 / 0.410 |

  Supporting results on other protocols:
  - FailBench (r4:19): best 0.77 mean balanced accuracy against the sources' "original outcome labels" (gap 0.23), and at
    most 0.60 on contact-rich assembly (gap at least 0.40). These are after-the-fact judges, not runtime monitors.
  - VLA-Scope (r4:67): ROC-AUC "0.8497 after 60 executed actions" on 1,400 out-of-distribution rollouts (gap 0.150).
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **The benchmark's failure is defined by a clock** (SAFE, r4:23). LIBERO stops a rollout when "a maximum rollout length
    is reached (considered a failure)", so "if a failure detector simply learns to count the time elapsed, i.e., st = t, it
    will achieve perfect failure detection". The authors truncate at the minimum length to remove that shortcut.
  - **Thresholds on the step clock.** SAFE uses "conformal prediction [58] to calibrate the threshold δt". Hide-and-Seek
    (r4:24) notes that "a fixed threshold fails to account for the natural temporal evolution of failure scores" and uses a
    time-varying band valid "Under the exchangeability assumption".
  - **Label weights on the step index.** SAFE-MLP uses "a linearly increasing temporal weight", which is "agnostic to when
    failure actually manifests".
  - **VLA-Scope** reads the risk at a fixed count of executed actions.
- **B-e.** No. Rollouts of 7B VLAs in LIBERO are GPU-scale. A toy policy with synthetic failures would run on a CPU as a
  proxy.

### r4-B4. Emergency stop of dynamically balancing (legged, humanoid) robots; the standards gap (open)

- **B-a.**
  - Siemens (Aug 2026; r4:25): "the safe state of a legged robot is an actively-controlled state, which violates the
    fail-passive assumption underlying ISO 13849-1 / EN 60204-1: removing power from a walking biped causes an uncontrolled
    fall, so classical de-energization is itself a hazard."
  - PRISM (Mar 2026; r4:26): "Emergency stops for humanoids cannot simply cut power; instead, a predefined fallback
    controller is triggered to drive the robot toward a minimum-risk condition (MRC)."
- **B-b.**
  - IEEE Humanoid Study Group (Sep 2025; r4:27): "no current standards account for robots that have unstable states as part
    of regular functioning"; "current SIL and PL measures assume deterministic systems". A new ISO TC 299 working group is
    "tasked to develop a safety standard (ISO/AWI 25785-1) for industrial mobile robots with actively controlled stability,
    notably including humanoids."
  - Siemens (Aug 2026; r4:28): "This fail-passive gap is why industrial humanoid deployment cannot yet reach end-to-end
    certification"; "motion-policy interruption and transition to a balanced standstill are not certified, no PFHD/DC/CCF is
    established, and no humanoid-specific standard governs them."
- **B-c** (Siemens, Tables II and III and results; r4:29). The target is the certified reference emergency stop: a Siemens
  fail-safe S7-1500 chain whose reaction subsystem is contactor power removal (Stop Category 0):
  > Reaction (2 contactors, Cat. 0) 1.45×10−9 e 7.30×10−9 3 Total 7.35×10−9 e 1.24×10−8 3

  That is PL e (ISO 13849-1) and SILCL 3 (IEC 62061). The humanoid result, on a Unitree G1 cell:
  - "with no such element the humanoid chain has no end-to-end PFHD yet";
  - measured stop time "0.3–1.0 s", worst-case response "≈1.1 s (dominated by tstop)";
  - a communication-loss standstill "(no topple) in 0.5–1.3 s in every trial" (four methods, three trials each).

  The gap is categorical, not a number: the reaction subsystem that closes the reference's PL e figure has no rated
  equivalent on the humanoid.

  The best learned result (PRISM Table IV, simulation; r4:30): at α = 0.47 the monitor flags 99.38% of unsafe states and
  calls 62.05% of safe states safe. The authors say this "fundamentally satisfies the stringent risk-reduction requirements
  of SIL 2". Their Siemens co-authors (r4:32) say such a data-driven monitor "does not by itself furnish a certifiable
  PFHD/DC argument", and a per-state accuracy is not a per-hour dangerous-failure rate. Recorded with that caveat.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Power removal (Cat. 0)** assumes a fail-passive safe state. The published industrial standards exclude the case: ISO
    10218-1:2025 does not cover "mobility when robots or manipulators are fixed to or part of mobile platforms" nor use "when
    the public, all ages or non-working adults have access" (r4:33). The IEEE report names the "unwritten assumption that
    the base ... is either fixed or has a statically stable base" (r4:34).
  - **Controlled stops and return to a safe state** (r4:34): "from immediate power removal to controlled stops, including
    moving back to a safe state".
  - **Learned safe-stoppability monitors** (PRISM, r4:31) assume that stops arrive at arbitrary times ("E-stop triggers are
    asynchronous and unpredictable. An emergency stop may be triggered at any time"). They use one fixed fallback policy
    assumed "well trained".
  - **Separation distance on the clock** (Siemens, r4:32). ISO 13855 uses one stopping time T, but "The outcome depends on
    gait phase". The result: "the ISO 13855 distance must be sized using the worst-case (single-support) tstop."
- **B-e.** Not as benchmarked: the named metrics are a functional-safety rating and times measured on a real cell. A
  planar-biped or inverted-pendulum walker with a fallback controller, with E-stops injected at random gait phases, runs on
  a CPU (numpy/scipy) as a proxy.
- **Standards status (what is public).**
  - **ISO 10218-1:2025 and -2:2025:** published; the free sample pages were read.
  - **ISO/TS 15066:** folded into ISO 10218-2:2025 (r4:66).
  - **ISO 25785-1:** at AWI stage in the Sep 2025 IEEE report. The iso.org page answered 403, so its current stage was not
    verified; a search-result title reads "ISO/CD 25785-1", which is not relied on.
  - **ISO 13482 revision:** a search snippet reports FDIS. iso.org answered 403 and the iTeh catalogue page rendered no text,
    so this is not verified.

### r4-B5. Formal verification of neural controllers at the scale of deployed policies (open; cross-source gap)

- **B-a** (ARCH-COMP26 AINNCS, 2026; r4:35): "it has been demonstrated that neural network verification is an NP-complete
  problem [37]."
- **B-b.**
  - VNN-COMP 2025 report (Dec 2025; r4:36): "neural network verification remains an open problem, despite significant
    efforts over the last years."
  - ARCH-COMP26 (r4:37): "we reused the same 12 benchmarks from last year, as several remain open"; the docking benchmark's
    "nonlinear safety specification causes the benchmark to remain unsolved one more year."
- **B-c.**
  - *Closed loop* (ARCH-COMP26; r4:38). The controllers "have no more than a thousand neurons and no more than 5 hidden
    layers". Unknown verdicts remain ("QUAD reach ? 9.517 Docking constraint ? 7.489" for JuliaReach), and CartPole is
    "challenging for most tools".
  - *Open loop* (VNN-COMP 2025; r4:40). The largest network verified "includes 138 million parameters" (VGG16).
  - *The target* is the policies deployed on robots: "OpenVLA provides an open 7B policy" (CertVLA; r4:39).
  - *The gap* is about six orders of magnitude in size for closed-loop verification (1e3 neurons against 7e9 parameters).
    These are different units and different sources, so this is an order-of-magnitude reading. The gap is about 50x for
    open-loop verification of a single network.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Reachability on a fixed control period** (r4:41). The loop is unrolled in sample-and-hold steps, and conservatism
    grows with the number of steps: "The high frequency of the control steps may lead to an increased conservativeness".
    The verdict depends on the time model: immrax, using "continuous feedback rather than the sample-and-hold
    architecture", reaches "different verification outcomes".
  - **Small feed-forward controllers** with low-dimensional state inputs (at most 12 inputs and 6 outputs).
  - **Runtime assurance instead of verification** (VLA survey; r4:42): "control barrier functions, reachability analysis,
    signal temporal logic" with learned uncertainty. The survey asks for "useful safety signals even under interruption
    (anytime safety)".
- **B-e.** Yes for the named benchmark. The ARCH-COMP benchmarks and tools (CORA, JuliaReach, CROWN-Reach, immrax) run on a
  CPU in seconds to minutes per instance; the 7B target does not.

### r4-B6. Cybersecurity of commercial robots and fleets (open; vendor-authored evidence)

- **B-a** (Alias Robotics and IOActive, Mar 2026; r4:43): consumer robots "are rapidly entering homes and workplaces, yet
  their security remains rooted in assumptions of specialized attacker expertise."
- **B-b.**
  - The same paper (r4:44): "while offensive capabilities have been democratized through AI, defensive measures often remain
    lagging behind."
  - Quadruped teleoperation survey (Feb 2026; r4:45): "a critical maturity gap between field-deployed communication
    protections (TRL 7–9) and largely experimental perception and operator-layer defenses (TRL 3– 5)".
- **B-c.**
  - *Consumer robots* (Table 1; r4:46). The assessment tool (CAI) "discovered in an automated manner 38 vulnerabilities" and
    "fleet-wide vulnerabilities and data protection violations affecting 267+ connected devices".

    | Robot | Vulnerabilities found | Assessment time |
    |---|---|---|
    | Hookii Neomow (lawnmower) | 9 | 2.5 h |
    | Hypershell X (exoskeleton) | 12 | 1.5 h |
    | HOBOT S7 Pro (window cleaner) | 17 | 3 h |

  - *Unitree G1 humanoid* (Sep 2025; r4:47). The authors call its security architecture "the most mature we have observed in
    commercial robotics", yet it is "exploitable using hardcoded AES keys shared across all units", with "effective entropy:
    0 bits due to fleet-wide key reuse across all devices".
  - *The target* is the EU Cyber Resilience Act as summarised by the European Commission (r4:48): manufacturers "shall
    ensure ... that vulnerabilities of that product, including its components, are handled effectively". Its "main
    obligations ... will apply from 11 December 2027" (r4:49). The CRA Annex I text (including its known-vulnerabilities
    requirement) could not be fetched from EUR-Lex, so a zero-vulnerability target is not quoted.
  - *The gap:* every assessed product had at least 9 findings. The assessments are by a security vendor with its own tool.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Intrusion detection** (r4:50) reaches "97– 99% accuracy on general ROS2/IoT traffic". Those results "are conditioned
    on datasets where traffic patterns are approximately stationary". The survey asks for "a phase-conditioned baseline that
    adapts to the robot’s current locomotion state ... rather than relying on a single global traffic model".
  - **Defence in depth on the host** (the Robot Immune System), with a proposed move to "GenAI-native defensive agents"
    (r4:51).
  - **Fleet-wide shared secrets** (r4:47): one key for every unit.
  - **Standards** add cybersecurity "to the extent that it applies to industrial robot safety" (ISO 10218-1:2025; r4:52).
- **B-e.** Not as benchmarked: the metric is an assessment of real products. A synthetic fleet or ROS 2 traffic model with
  gait-phase-modulated messages and injected attacks runs on a CPU as a proxy.

### r4-B7. Privacy of robots' sensor data and physical-world privacy awareness (open)

- **B-a** (position paper, ICML 2026; r4:53): embodied AI has advanced "without considering their coupled privacy
  implications in high-frequency deployments where privacy leakage is often irreversible."
- **B-b.**
  - SoK (Jun 2026; r4:54): "privacy risks and mitigation strategies remain comparatively underexplored."
  - Position paper (r4:55): "current privacy-aware EAI solutions remain largely stage-local and disjointed."
- **B-c.**
  - *EAPrivacy* (ICLR 2026; r4:56). In Tier 2, the task is to select the action humans rated most appropriate; the best model
    reaches "only 59% accuracy in scenarios involving changing physical environments", a gap of 41 points. In Tier 3, the
    target is an action plan that "respects the privacy of a secret item while still completing the task"; the best models "achieve the
    lowest violation rates of 71%", a gap of 71 points to no violation. Models "prioritized completion over the constraint
    in up to 86% of cases."
  - *Deployed consumer robots* (r4:57). "None of the three robots were found to implement consent management or data
    subject rights mechanisms consistent with GDPR requirements." All 18 GDPR-related API endpoint patterns returned HTTP
    404.
  - *Unitree G1* (r4:58): telemetry sent "every 300 seconds without operator notice".

### r4-B8. Collaborative throughput lost to safety speed reduction (open; the oracle overstates the recoverable gap)

- **B-a** (Faroni et al., IEEE RA-L, Dec 2025; r4:60): "Ensuring human safety in collaborative robotics can compromise
  efficiency because traditional safety measures increase robot cycle time when human interaction is frequent"; SSM and
  PFL "ensure safety by applying speed reduction rules. However, the speed reduction significantly impacts robot cycle
  times".
- **B-b** (Bricher & Müller, Sensors, Nov 2025; r4:61): "current ISO/TS-15066-compliant implementations often limit the
  efficiency of collaborative tasks due to conservative speed restrictions." Their perception-based alternative still needs
  "functional safety certification".
- **B-c** (Faroni et al., Table III; r4:62). The setting is a real UR5e pick-and-packaging cell with a human operator. The
  robot's speed is "the nominal speed (i.e., without human presence) multiplied by s(xr, xh)".

  | Method | Mean speed scaling s (variant 1 / variant 2) | Nominal speed lost |
  |---|---|---|
  | Greedy learned scheduling (best) | 0.74 / 0.68 | 26% / 32% |
  | Random task choice | 0.60 / 0.59 | 40% / 41% |

  The oracle s = 1 is an upper bound: no safe cell reaches it with a person near, so the recoverable part is smaller and the
  source does not quantify it. A second paper (r4:63) reports "a quantitative reduction in cycle time of up to 15% compared
  to conventional safety technology" (a single subject, per its authors).
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Static safety zones** "cause robots to slow down or stop when a human enters a predefined area" (r4:64): boundaries
    fixed at installation.
  - **Worst-case constant speed** (PFL practice; r4:65): "adjusted to a constant value that is in accordance with the most
    restrictive collision situation".
  - **Learned scheduling** uses a predictive window fixed on the clock: "we set w equal to the average duration of the
    robot’s actions (∼14 s)" (r4:64).
  - **The standards route** (r4:66): ISO 10218-2:2025 incorporates "the content of ISO/TS 15066"; its safety functions are
    PFL or protective devices.
- **B-e.** Not as benchmarked (a real cell with a human operator). A simulated shared-workspace scheduling model with a
  stepwise speed-scaling function (as in the paper's own simulation, Table II) runs on a CPU in minutes, as a proxy.
