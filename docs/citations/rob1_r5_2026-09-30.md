# ROB1 citation checks, family R5: news and industry feeds (2026-09-30; R10)

This is stage 1 of `Robotics/DECLARATION.md` (pushed at b61e0a9), family **R5**: deployment bottlenecks reported in 2025-26
for humanoids, warehouse and logistics robots, field robots and delivery drones. The scope covers:
- reliability, uptime and failures in production;
- battery life against a shift;
- cost and the business case;
- dependence on remote human help (teleoperation);
- the cost of collecting training data;
- safety incidents.

The dossier records bottlenecks only. It makes no CRR reading; that is stage 2. The double check (stage 1b) is done by a
different agent.

**The rule for news.** News (The Robot Report, IEEE Spectrum, CNBC, TechCrunch, trade sites) was used as a pointer only.
A claim enters only with a primary source, and every quote comes from that primary source. The primary sources are:
- **company filings:** SEC Forms S-4 and 10-K (fetched from EDGAR), an HKEX annual results announcement, and a reply filed
  with the Shanghai Stock Exchange;
- **company statements:** press releases and product pages;
- **the International Federation of Robotics (IFR):** the humanoid position-paper infographic, and the World Robotics 2025
  and 2026 press-conference slides;
- **a regulator (FAA):** the Part 108 NPRM and a Part 107 certificate of waiver;
- **an investigator (NTSB):** final and preliminary reports;
- **a US Senator's published investigation;**
- **papers:** arXiv, current version.

**How the sources were handled.** Every source was fetched on the day (2026-09-30) through the session proxy, with `curl`
and TLS verification on. Text was extracted as follows:
- **PDFs:** pymupdf 1.28.2 (`page.get_text()`).
- **HTML:** BeautifulSoup 4 `get_text('\n')` after removing script, style, noscript and svg; runs of spaces and blank lines
  were collapsed.

Quotes are copied from that text. Only whitespace is normalised (and hyphens at line breaks rejoined by the verifier). Table
rows and slide text are quoted as the extractor emitted them. Five quotes use "[...]" (r5:13, r5:24, r5:27, r5:31, r5:32).
Each "[...]" skips either a slide bullet glyph (U+F0A7) or a line break that the extractor turned into a space inside Chinese
text.

Fetched files are held outside the repository, under `/tmp/claude-0/rob_src/r5/`:
- `src/`: the fetched files;
- `txt/`: the extracted text;
- `search/queries.txt`: the search log, the EDGAR queries and the list of direct fetches;
- `search/unreachable.txt`: the failures.

Their sha256 is recorded below and in `/tmp/claude-0/rob_src/r5/SHA256SUMS.txt` (117 files).

**Verification.** The claims are in `Robotics/checks/claims_r5.py`: 74 claims and 144 quotes, over 8 bottlenecks. They were
checked with a scratch copy of `Open_Bottlenecks/checks/verify.py`, pointed at `claims_r5.py` with root
`/tmp/claude-0/rob_src`. The family's pinned check will be `Robotics/checks/verify.py`, which was not written here. The
scratch script and its output are saved as `/tmp/claude-0/rob_src/r5/verify_r5_scratch.py` and `verify_r5_scratch.txt`.
- **Result:** "quotes found verbatim: 144 of 144 (claims 74; raw files 32, missing 0); not found 0; missing 0".
- **Negative control:** one number was altered (the robot stow rate "224 UPH" changed to "234 UPH" in r5:36). The check then
  read 143 of 144, with r5:36 quote 2 NOT FOUND, as it should (`verify_r5_negative_control.txt`).
- **Drafting failures, corrected before the file was finalised:**
  - three slide quotes failed on the bullet glyph and were split with "[...]" (r5:13, r5:32);
  - eight notes relied on facts outside their quotes. The facts were either added as quotes (r5:27, r5:48, r5:59, r5:63, and
    the new claim r5:73 for r5:60) or removed from the notes (r5:25, r5:38, r5:58).

**Rules for the numbers.**
- Every number below is the source's own, on its own protocol.
- The gaps are the agent's arithmetic on quoted numbers. No currency is converted (no exchange rate was quoted).
- Numbers from different sources are not comparable. Where a target and a best result come from different documents, this
  is flagged: r5-B1 (Agility's runtime against the BMW shift) and r5-B2 (Agility's accuracy against Figure's target).
- **Company statements and filings are the company's own figures.** They are not independent measurements. A filing's unit
  economics are "Unaudited Prospective" management estimates, and announced specifications (Digit 5) describe robots not yet
  shipped.
- **Claims that contradict a bottleneck are kept and marked CONTRARY** in their note, for the double check: r5:8 (UBTech's
  24-hour operation), r5:27 (Unitree's low price and margin) and r5:58 (Starship's "over 99% autonomy").
- A description of a mechanism or setting is the agent's reading of the fetched text. The quotes are the evidence.
- Code and data links were **not checked**.

## Bottlenecks

The open test: B-a, B-b and B-c must all be present (the stage-1b table computes it from the roles). Five are read as open
(r5-B1 to r5-B5), and three as **not shown open** (r5-B6, r5-B7, r5-B8), because no source gives a numeric target. Two of
the open ones carry a note that the double check may find them contested:
- r5-B1, by UBTech's claim of 24-hour operation through battery swapping;
- r5-B3, on price, by Unitree's low and profitable unit price.

r5-B6, which is not shown open, also carries a contrary vendor claim: Starship says "over 99% autonomy" for sidewalk robots.

**Not formed as bottlenecks** (in scope, but no 2025-26 primary source with a best result against a stated target was found
in this sweep; "not found" is not "solved"):
- humanoid safety incidents (the IFR names "falling and power failure"). These are covered by family R4 (r4-B4, the emergency
  stop of balancing robots);
- delivery-drone endurance, which is family R3's r3-B1;
- the cost per hour of teleoperated data. Only vendor blogs gave per-hour figures, and they were not used.

### r5-B1. Humanoid battery runtime against a working shift and a 24-hour day (open; may be contested)

- **B-a** (IFR infographic, Jul 2025; r5:1): "Battery power is a fundamental challenge for humanoid robots."
- **B-b.**
  - The same IFR document (2025; r5:2): "So far, a battery cycle does not last a full working day."
  - The Agility S-4 (4 Sep 2026; r5:3): customers want to "maximize utilization across multiple shifts", which Digit v5 "was
    engineered to address".
- **B-c.** There are two metrics.
  - **Runtime per charge, against the customer's shift.**
    - Best deployed: Digit v4 lasts "up to four hours" (Agility, 31 Mar 2025; r5:4). The NEO specification also gives a
      run-time of 4h (r5:6).
    - Target: the BMW line ran a "10-hour shift Monday-Friday" (Figure, 19 Nov 2025; r5:7).
    - Gap: 4/10 = 40% of a shift per charge. This is **cross-source**.
  - **Productive hours in 24.**
    - Deployed: Digit 4's 2:1 run-to-charge gives at most 16 h (67%).
    - Announced: Digit 5 has a "90-minute runtime battery", charges in 9 minutes (10:1), and claims "more than 20 hours of
      productive work in a 24-hour day", at least 83% (15 Sep 2026; r5:5). Early access is in H1 2027.
  - **CONTRARY at the day level** (r5:8): UBTech's 2025 results claim a "3-minute autonomous battery swap and 24-hour
    continuous operation", on a robot delivered at the "1,000-unit level". It is a company claim with no measured hours
    worked. The per-cycle reading (IFR) stands.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Opportunity charging at a dock or mat** (Figure 03, r5:10): "near-continuous operation as long as it can step onto a
    charging mat for a certain period of time during the use case", and data offload "during shift breaks". It assumes the
    workflow's clock leaves charging windows.
  - **Fast charging (10:1)** (Digit 5, r5:5). Runtime per charge falls from 4 h to 90 min, so there are more energy
    boundaries per day.
  - **Autonomous hot-swap** (UBTech, r5:9). The robot chooses "battery swap or charge mode according to the task
    priorities", a state-triggered boundary. It needs swap stations and spare packs.
  - **Self-docking on need in the home** (1X, r5:11).
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. Runtime and availability are measured on hardware in service. A duty-cycle simulation would test
  scheduling, not battery capacity.

### r5-B2. Humanoid functional reliability in production (open)

- **B-a** (IFR infographic, 2025; r5:12): "Humanoids will not compete with traditional industrial robots in terms of speed,
  precision, reliability and repeatability."
- **B-b.**
  - IFR, World Robotics 2026 slides (24 Sep 2026; r5:13): "Technology yet to prove reliability and efficiency". "Safety and
    functional reliability" is among the "Major challenges".
  - The Agility S-4 (2026; r5:14): "the useful life and durability of Digit units" "remain unproven", and "Agility has no
    experience maintaining or servicing its products at large scale."
  - Figure (Nov 2025; r5:15): the forearm was "our top hardware failure point at BMW".
- **B-c.**
  - **Best reported:** about 98% "operational accuracy" for Digit v4 at Schaeffler (about 25,000 totes) and at GXO (more
    than 100,000 totes), over more than 65,000 hours of commercial operation (S-4; r5:16). The term is not defined.
  - **Target:** the BMW line's KPIs for Figure 02 (r5:17): "> 99% success per shift", interventions "zero per shift", and an 84 s
    cycle. Figure logged interventions but reports **no achieved value** for any KPI.
  - **Gap (cross-source, different tasks):** a failure share of about 2% against less than 1%, at least twice the target's.
    Interventions per shift, uptime and MTBF are disclosed by no source.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Field calibration per robot** (r5:18).
  - **Redesign at hardware-generation boundaries:** the wrist electronics were re-architected for Figure 03, and the Figure 02
    fleet was retired.
  - **Vendor-owned fleets (RaaS) with time-based KPIs** ("uptime, throughput and Mean Time Between Incidents", r5:19). The
    values are not published.
  - **Human pause or reset as the recovery path**, counted per shift.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. The metric is measured on physical robots in customer plants.

### r5-B3. Unit cost and business case of humanoids for productive work (open; price part may be contested)

- **B-a** (IFR infographic, 2025; r5:20): "There is not yet a mass production reaching economies of scale regarding costs."
- **B-b.**
  - IFR, World Robotics 2026 (r5:21): "Lack of economic viable business case". Also: humanoids are "still on the verge of
    making the leap from laboratories to pilots – let alone real deployments", with "Nearly 7,000" full-size humanoids sold in
    2025.
  - Tesla 10-K (29 Jan 2026; r5:22): "We have yet to commercialize Bots".
  - The Agility S-4 (r5:23): "Agility’s RaaS model has not yet demonstrated profitability at scale", and "Gross margins
    remained negative".
  - Unitree's reply to the SSE (20 Mar 2026; r5:24): "在工业场景尚未形成规模化应用". In the agent's translation: industrial
    use is not yet at scale.
- **B-c.**
  - **The bill of materials of an industrial humanoid** (S-4; r5:25). It is "approximately $150,000 at commercial launch".
    Agility's own targets are "approximately $75,000" at 1,000 robots a year and "approximately $30,000" at 10,000 a year.
    The gap is **2.0x and 5.0x** (same document).
  - **Agility's 2025 financials** (r5:26): cost of goods sold $4.5 million against net sales of $0.7 million plus $1.1
    million. That is 2.5x sales.
  - **CONTRARY on price** (r5:27). Unitree's humanoid unit price was RMB 167,600 (16.76 万元) at a 62.91% gross margin
    (Jan-Sep 2025). But 73.60% of its humanoid revenue came from research and education, and 9.01% from industry
    applications. Explicit productive scenes accounted for RMB 15.702 million, **2.64%** of humanoid revenue (agent's
    arithmetic).
  - **UBTech** (r5:28): about RMB 820 million from 1,079 units, about RMB 760,000 per unit including services (agent's
    arithmetic), at a "high-margin".
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Volume cost-down** (Figure's BotQ: "up to 12,000 humanoid robots per year", and a goal of "100,000 robots over the next
    four years", r5:30). It assumes the demand arrives.
  - **RaaS** (r5:29). The monthly fee is $8,500, priced "at a discount to a customer’s fully burdened labor cost". Payback of
    "approximately 1.1 years" is a planning assumption, and the vendor carries the durability risk.
  - **Low-price volume** (Unitree, r5:31). Margin is traded for adoption.
  - **The IFR's view for logistics robots** (r5:32): "High upfront investment costs" are a major challenge, and "RaaS business
    models lower adoption barriers considerably".
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable: these are business data.

### r5-B4. Warehouse robotic stow and pick: coverage, rate and defects against the production target (open)

- **B-a** (Amazon Robotics, Stow, arXiv 2505.04572 v1, 7 May 2025; r5:33): "strict business requirements for high producing
  rates and low defect generation have prohibited warehouse robotics from performing this task". The task "is currently
  performed manually more than 14 billion times per year."
- **B-b.**
  - The same paper (r5:34): unproductive stows "will aim for much lower rates (i.e. 3% vs. 9%)", and one behaviour "needs to
    be changed as the system scales".
  - Amazon's Vulcan page (published 7 May 2025, modified 4 Jun 2026; r5:35): the robot identifies "when it can’t move a
    specific item, and can ask a human partner to tag in".
- **B-c** (same paper, same deployment; r5:36).
  - **Design target:** "80% of items in the warehouse at a rate of 300 units per hour", more than "20 hr per day and 7 days per
    week".
  - **Result:** in March 2025, robots stowed at 224 UPH, against humans on the same floor at 243 UPH. That is **92.2% of the
    human rate and 74.7% of the design rate**.
  - **Outcomes over 100,000 attempts:** 85.86% successful, 9.31% unproductive (against the 3% aim, **3.1x**), and 4.83% defects.
  - **Coverage** (Vulcan page; r5:37): "approximately 75% of all various types of items", against the 80% design. The
    denominators may differ.
  - Hours per day and interventions are not reported.
  - Supporting only (a different task, no target): Ocado's best site picks "c.50% volumes robotically" (r5:38).
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Human-in-the-loop decomposition** (r5:39): "a human checks inbound items for quality and feeds three robotic
    workcells".
  - **A learned risk model with a fixed exploration rate** (r5:40): "up to 5% of the time, the system relaxes margins and/or
    randomly chooses a behavior". It has "a cost of needing to continually recollect training data as development evolves and
    behaviors change", and exploring is "critical for continual learning".
  - **Real-robot data** (r5:41). Simulation was judged insufficient.
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. The benchmark is a production warehouse; only a toy proxy would run on a CPU.

### r5-B5. Field harvesting robots: throughput and success against human pickers (open)

- **B-a** (Zhu et al., arXiv 2606.14089 v1, 12 Jun 2026; r5:42): "low throughput and poor performance in challenging orchard
  environments hinder its commercial adoption".
- **B-b.**
  - CLASP (arXiv 2609.18051 v1, 16 Sep 2026; r5:43): "harvesting for the fresh market remains almost entirely manual".
  - Zhu et al. (arXiv 2506.05714 v1, 6 Jun 2025; r5:44): "current systems still fall short of the speed and accuracy required
    for practical deployment."
- **B-c** (CLASP, with the human baseline measured in the same field; r5:45).
  - The gripper achieved "32 berries per minute against 55 for a human picker", i.e. **58%**. This was with the gripper
    manually aligned; the rate of the autonomous pipeline is not reported.
  - Autonomous success was 23 of 25 (92%). The authors say this "should not be interpreted as a comprehensive reliability
    estimate".
  - Firmness was 15% below hand-picked fruit.
  - Supporting only (no human baseline in the paper): the apple robot reached an "80.0% per-attempt success rate and a mean
    per-arm cycle time of 7.53 s" over 1,738 arm cycles (r5:46).
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Stop-and-go harvesting** (r5:47): the robot runs "under human operator control", and "a human operator drives the tractor
    and performs platform adjustments at each harvesting stop". The authors propose repositioning "according to fruit
    distribution rather than halting at fixed intervals".
  - **Teleoperated transport between fixed test locations**, with perception from a fixed viewpoint that sees 40.3% of
    clusters (r5:48).
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable. Field throughput needs orchards and hardware.

### r5-B6. Remote human help per robot (teleoperation dependence) (not shown open)

- **B-a.**
  - The Agility S-4 (r5:49): "Many companies in the humanoid robotics industry have not yet achieved autonomous commercial
    deployment, relying instead on teleoperation, demonstrations or prototype systems."
  - The FAA Part 108 NPRM (7 Aug 2025; r5:50): controlling "multiple UA per person" is "an important consideration in scaling
    UAS operations".
- **B-b.**
  - The NPRM (r5:51) proposes a "ratio of 1:1" by default, and says that "1:many at scale" is wanted, but "at this time there
    is limited industry standardization".
  - Serve's 10-K (Mar 2026; r5:52): "all automated delivery robots still require a certain amount of human involvement".
  - Senator Markey's release (31 Mar 2026; r5:53): "Every AV company refused to disclose how frequently their RAOs intervene".
    This is a road-vehicle comparator.
  - 1X (28 Oct 2025; r5:54): owners "can schedule a 1X Expert to guide it through unknown tasks".
- **B-c: none.** No source states a numeric target, and the natural metric (interventions per robot-hour) is withheld. The
  numbers found are recorded as B-d:
  - "up to 6 sUA" per remote pilot by FAA waiver (Zipline, Jan 2025; r5:56);
  - about 70 remote-assistance agents on duty for over 3,000 vehicles (Waymo, Feb 2026; r5:57);
  - 547 daily active robots against about 2,000 deployed (Serve, Q4 2025; r5:59);
  - **CONTRARY:** Starship's "over 99% autonomy" (undefined metric; r5:58).
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Scheduled teleoperation sessions booked on the calendar** (1X Expert Mode, r5:55). They double as data collection.
  - **Continuous remote monitoring with situation-triggered assists** (Serve, r5:59). It is partly provided by offshore
    vendors.
  - **Event-driven assistance requested by the robot** (Waymo; Starship's assistants "on standby").
  - **Ratios raised by waiver**, with redundancy and one operational volume per pilot (r5:56).
  - **System-level supervision:** "pilots oversee the whole system" (Wing, r5:74; the ratio is not stated).
- **B-e.** CPU-testable as a queueing or fleet model (robot-initiated help against scheduled or continuous monitoring), in
  minutes. No public intervention data exist to calibrate it.

### r5-B7. Delivery-drone collisions with obstacles and other aircraft (not shown open)

- **B-a.** These are incident records, not rates.
  - **NTSB WPR26LA001 and WPR26LA002** (preliminary; 1 Oct 2025; r5:60, r5:73). Two Amazon MK30 delivery drones on Part 135
    flights struck the same mobile crane, at 0949 and 0958. The crane had been "erected between 0730 and 0800".
  - **NTSB WPR25LA103** (final, 9 May 2025; r5:62). Probable cause: "failure to maintain clearance from an obstacle".
  - **NTSB WPR24LA302** (final, 13 Mar 2025; r5:63). Two drones "diverted ... to the same alternate landing pad", and a mid-air
    collision followed.
- **B-b: none from 2025-26** stating the problem open. **B-c: none:** no rate against a target was found, and the NPRM sets no
  obstacle-data refresh interval.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Obstacle survey on a fixed clock** (r5:61). Amazon's crews "conduct rooftop scans of the surrounding area twice per
    day". The last scan was at 0658, before the crane went up.
  - **Obstacle assessment once before operations** in a new area (NPRM § 108.165(e), r5:64; the Zipline waiver's site survey,
    r5:65).
  - **Shared contingency rules** across drones (r5:63).
- **B-e.** CPU-testable as a simulation of obstacle arrival against scan schedules. No incident-rate data are public to
  calibrate it.

### r5-B8. Cost of collecting robot training data (not shown open here; the size gap is r2-B6)

- **B-a.**
  - IFR, World Robotics 2026 (r5:66): "Lack of training data" is a major challenge for humanoids.
  - HumanScale (arXiv 2606.20521 v1, 18 Jun 2026; r5:68): the public robot data total "∼2 × 104 hours", which is 10^4 with
    the superscript lost in extraction. "The low-cost ALOHA platform costs ∼$20k per station".
- **B-b** (HumanScale; r5:67): the scalability of teleoperated data "is limited by high collection cost, acquisition
  difficulty, and low behavioral and environmental diversity".
- **B-c: none in this family.** No 2025-26 primary source gives a data-collection cost against a target. The size gap
  against Goldberg's LLM-equivalent target is family R2's r2-B6, and is not counted again.
- **B-d.** The method families, and the assumption each makes:
  - **Teleoperated collection counted in hours.** An hour of teleoperation holds fewer trajectories (about 8,000 against
    45,000 per 100 h; r5:69).
  - **Pretraining on human video**, then adapting on a small robot set.
  - **Demonstration plus simulation RL before deployment, then fleet learning** (Agility, r5:70).
  - **Fleet data offload at docks** (Figure, r5:71), and **dedicated data-collection centres** (UBTech, r5:72).
- **B-e.** Not CPU-testable: data cost is an operational figure.
