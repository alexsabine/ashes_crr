# ROB1 stage 3, candidate C-R1, searcher A: prior art for a phase-keyed stop of a dynamically balancing legged robot (2026-09-30; R10)

Stage 3 of `Robotics/DECLARATION.md`, candidate **C-R1** of `Robotics/CRR_READING.md` (bottleneck r4-B4, emergency stop of
dynamically balancing robots). The search was for the **specific mechanism**, before any code. This is searcher A's dossier.

**The mechanism searched (CRR_READING.md, C-R1).**
- A stop (emergency stop, protective stop, safe stop, fall-safe stop) of a dynamically balancing legged or humanoid robot that
  is **initiated, timed or shaped by the gait's own phase**. The caller's instances: gait-phase-dependent stopping,
  phase-aware stop trajectories, capture-point or stance-phase stopping, "stop at the next stance", phase-dependent
  safe-state selection.
- The frontier comparator (stage 1, harvester r4:32): **one stopping time on the clock, sized by the worst gait phase**
  (ISO 13855, S = K·T + C, with T the worst-case single-support stopping time); the stop can arrive at any instant; one fixed
  fallback policy.
- CRR's prediction: over stop commands arriving uniformly in time, a stop keyed to the gait's own phase has a lower worst case
  and a lower mean of stopping distance and falls, at equal delay budget, than the best single clock-sized stop. The
  must-fail case: a statically stable (fixed-base) system with no phase, where the two tie.

**Positions** (the caller's):
- **P1:** phase-dependent timing or shaping of a stop for a legged or humanoid robot is published.
- **P2:** it is shown to reduce worst-case or mean stopping distance, time or falls against a phase-independent stop.
- **P3:** it appears in a standard, a standards proposal or an industrial safety function (for example ISO 25785-1 drafts,
  IFR or industry white papers).

**Reading tags** (fixed before the readings; full text in `Robotics/checks/claims_s3_r1a.py`).
- **states:**
  - P1: the source designs, implements or specifies a stop of a walking or balancing legged robot (emergency, protective, safe
    or commanded) whose initiation, timing or shape is set by where the gait is in its cycle: the support phase, the swing
    state, the next touchdown or apex, or the capture point against the base of support.
  - P2: a measured comparison, on hardware or in simulation, in which such a stop gives fewer falls, or a shorter stopping
    distance or time, than a phase-independent stop (immediate or clock-timed).
  - P3: a standard, a standards draft or proposal, or an industrial safety function specifies phase-keyed timing or shaping.
- **close:**
  - P1: the phase dependence of stopping is stated but no phase-keyed stop is designed; or the stop is conditioned on the
    robot's state but not keyed to the gait phase (learned stop policies, stoppability estimators, capturability constraints
    under a clock-timed stop); or an operational stop is gated by the support state with a clock hysteresis; or the mechanism
    is shown in humans, not robots.
  - P2: a state-dependent (not phase-keyed) stop decision compared with an unconditioned stop; or a qualitative claim of
    shorter stops with no measured phase-independent baseline.
  - P3: a controlled (non-de-energising) stop or a stance- or state-dependent safe state for legged robots, specified without
    phase-keyed timing; or a manufacturer's patent of a phase-keyed stop with no evidence that it is a deployed or certified
    function.
- **contradicts:**
  - P1 and P2: the source shows a phase-keyed stop infeasible, or no better or worse than a phase-independent stop.
  - P3: the source documents a standard, a standards route or an industrial safety function that sizes or budgets the stop by
    one clock time independent of the gait phase. This is evidence against P3 for that standard or function only; it cannot
    disprove existence elsewhere.

## A. Phase-keyed emergency stops of walking humanoids (1992-2007)

### 1. Emergency stop control system for mobile robot (Takahashi & Suzaki, Honda)

- **Identifier:** US Patent 5,369,346 A, granted 29 Nov 1994; filed 20 May 1993; priority JP 22 May 1992; assignee Honda
  Giken Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha.
- **Integrity:** `raw/US5369346.pdf` sha256 a278a751c9a7399f1e8772613608ce0795e4102f783352dbc9360d293fea59e6 (USPTO image PDF,
  14 pages).
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/US5369346_ocr.txt` (OCR of pages 11-14).

> Further, if the robot is a legged mobile robot, its attitude is intrinsically unstable owing to the large changes that take place in the support polygon. The collision of such a robot with persons and objects cannot be reliably avoided simply by stopping it.

> It can thus be understood from the figure that the attitude of the biped walking robot is particularly unstable during one-leg support period.

> or not the ZMP is within the stable regions, whereby it [...] is determined whether or not the robot will be able to maintain its attitude in the stopped state if the emergency stop is executed.

> If the result of the determination is affirmative, control passes directly to step S118 in which walking or the robot motion is stopped, and if it [...] is negative, control passes to step S120 in which the walking speed is reduced and control is then returned to step S116.

> Steps 116 and 120 are then repeatedly executed until it is confirmed that a stable attitude has been reached (e.g. that the center of gravity is at the bottom [...] of the supporting foot), and control then passes to step S118.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:1).
  - On an emergency-stop signal the biped is not stopped at once. The controller first asks whether the current attitude can
    hold a stopped state: the centre of gravity over the supporting foot, or the ZMP inside the stable region, which the
    patent says differs greatly between one-leg and two-leg support.
  - If it cannot, the robot keeps walking at reduced speed until it can, and only then stops. The stop is timed by the gait's
    own state, not by a clock.
  - It is not compared with an immediate or a clock-timed stop.
- **Reading: close P3** (r1a:19). A manufacturer patented a phase-keyed emergency stop in 1994. The sources found do not show
  it as a deployed or certified safety function. An investigator who counts a manufacturer's patented safety function as "an
  industrial safety function" would read this **states**.

### 2. Emergency stop algorithm for walking humanoid robots (Morisawa, Kajita, Harada, Fujiwara, Kanehiro, Kaneko & Hirukawa, AIST)

- **Identifier:** IEEE/RSJ IROS 2005, pp. 31-36, DOI 10.1109/IROS.2005.1544955. Conference PDF (IEEE copyright line on p. 31),
  copy hosted by scispace.com.
- **Integrity:** `raw/morisawa2005_scispace.pdf` sha256 3253cafc4d799c927f36cbecf53f400023216e79c362297d85c96d911a13dc36.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/morisawa2005_scispace.txt`

> Since an emergency occurs at unpredictable timing and at any state of robot, the stopping motion must be generated in real-time.

> During the single support phase, a landing time and position are determined by evaluating the average velocity of the swing leg and the horizontal position of the COG. During the double support phase, the travel distance of the COG and the ZMP are evaluated.

> If the emergency signal is given in double support phase, the stop motion can also deal with the emergency by starting from ZMP Transition Phase.

> The stop motion generator postponed the touchdown time and extended the step length.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:2).
  - The emergency stop of HRP-2 is **shaped by the gait phase at which the signal arrives**. From single support it begins with
    a Landing phase, whose touchdown time and position are re-planned. From double support it begins at the ZMP Transition
    phase.
  - The stop is completed within one step.
  - It is shown in simulation (from late single support and from double support) and on hardware (from mid single support).
    There is no table of stopping distance across phases.

> However, these methods require more than two steps to stop the robot.

> In this paper, we propose a method to generate emergency stop motion in real-time which can lead the robot to stop within one step.

- **Reading: close P2** (r1a:3).
  - This is a qualitative claim against earlier real-time pattern generators, with no measured phase-independent baseline.
  - It also carries a warning. One of the earlier methods is Nishiwaki's, which connects patterns "every one step", that is,
    only at the gait's own step boundaries. Waiting for the gait's own boundary is itself named as the slow way to stop. The
    gain here comes from **re-planning the current step from any instant**, not from deferring the stop to a phase.

### 3. Emergent stop for Humanoid Robots (Tanaka, Takubo, Inoue & Arai, Osaka University)

- **Identifier:** IEEE/RSJ IROS 2006, DOI 10.1109/IROS.2006.281833. **Abstract only** (OpenAlex; full text not reached).
- **Integrity:** `raw/openalex_10.1109_iros.2006.281833.json` sha256
  2fb20c4c6923c791b0c35cf66950fa6df5070dc483771b1271e6db535c0dbbc1.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/openalex_10.1109_iros.2006.281833.txt`

> The stable gait change is generated by adjusting the amount of the ZMP modification according to the timing of stop command.

> In this method, the humanoid robot can stop immediately within one step to avoid a collision

> The stop motion is typically divided two mode; single leg stop motion and double leg stop motion. The stop mode and the next landing position are decided according to the command time of the stop signal.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:4). The stop of a walking HRP-2 is keyed to **where in the gait cycle the command arrives**. The
  stop mode (single-leg or double-leg) and the next landing position are chosen from the command time, using a precomputed map
  of ZMP modification against command timing.

### 4. Emergent walking stop using 3-D ZMP modification criteria map for humanoid robot (Takubo, Tanaka, Inoue & Arai)

- **Identifier:** IEEE ICRA 2007, pp. 2676-2681, DOI 10.1109/ROBOT.2007.363869. **Abstract only** (OpenAlex; full text not
  reached).
- **Integrity:** `raw/openalex_10.1109_robot.2007.363869.json` sha256
  9eed91a9527aebc327bc6cdc0cb94f047926cab52bc566b53f9d429f635d656e.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/openalex_10.1109_robot.2007.363869.txt`

> We make the map of relation among the ZMP modification length, the modification timing and the timing of the stop command for stable gait modification.

> In the single leg support phase, the next landing position and timing are decided according to command time of the stop signal. In the double leg support phase, the humanoid robot can stop anytime without changing standing position.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:5). This is the successor of source 3. The stop's landing position and timing are set by the gait
  phase at the command (single-leg support); in double support the robot stops at once. This is the phase-keyed shaping of the
  stop that C-R1 predicts, published in 2006-07 on HRP-2. Agility's 2026 patent (source 12) cites this paper among its
  references.

### 5. Capture Point: A Step toward Humanoid Push Recovery (Pratt, Carff, Drakunov & Goswami)

- **Identifier:** IEEE-RAS Humanoids 2006, pp. 200-207, DOI 10.1109/ICHR.2006.321385. Conference PDF, copy hosted by
  scispace.com.
- **Integrity:** `raw/pratt2006_capture_point.pdf` sha256 5f1f096fee94684873d787497d92b8f297d6f7b84c9215cac47a15e67e340ada.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/pratt2006_capture_point.txt`

> we present methods for computing Capture Points and the Capture Region, the region on the ground where a humanoid must step to in order to come to a complete stop. The intersection between the Capture Region and the Base of Support determines which strategy the robot should adopt to successfully stop in a given situation.

> The robot is commanded to stop shortly after taking a step at about 3.9 seconds. It does so by lunging its upper body, without taking an additional step.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:6), under the caller's list, which names capture-point stopping as an instance of C-R1.
  - How a walking biped stops (balance in place, lunge, or step, and where) is chosen from its current state against the base
    of support. A commanded stop is simulated.
  - The quantity is the capture point, a state, not a gait-phase variable. If the investigator reads only explicit
    phase-keying as C-R1, this is **close**.

## B. A phase-timed stop measured against an immediate one (2023)

### 6. Optimizing Bipedal Locomotion for The 100m Dash With Comparison to Human Running (Crowley, Dao, Duan, Green, Hurst & Fern)

- **Identifier:** arXiv:2508.03070, **v1** (5 Aug 2025; the only version). Comment: published by IEEE at ICRA 2023, pp.
  12205-12211. Cassie, Oregon State University.
- **Integrity:** `raw/2508.03070v1.pdf` sha256 b9333617cccd99e452676bf7ca73bfa24b8310ebb2c633ebc883906b6286ac2f.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/2508.03070v1.txt`

> Instead when the controller receives the signal to stand it [...] waits until the phase is in a good position in the gait cycle and then swaps to the standing policy.

> When Cassie receives the signal to transition from stepping to standing, the controller waits for either of these two phases and swaps policies at that moment.

- **Reading: states P1** (r1a:7), in its **timing** form. A stop command arriving at any time is deferred to the gait's own
  phase (either foot at its apex, read from the policy's clock) before the running policy is replaced by a standing policy. It
  is an operational stop at the end of a record run, not a safety function.

> Prior to these adjustments the controller made the transition from stepping to standing immediately when given the signal to swap. This setup was severely unreliable and almost always failed, both on hardware and in simulation (less than 10% success rate).

> After making the controller wait for an apex phase the success rate soared to about 2 in 3 attempts, and with this final adjustment to the speed command it improved beyond expectations to 100% of >20 trials.

- **Reading: states P2** (r1a:8), on falls.
  - On Cassie, on hardware and in simulation, the phase-timed stop took success from under 10% (immediate,
    phase-independent swap) to about 2 in 3 (apex-timed).
  - With a speed-command adjustment added, it reached 100% of more than 20 trials.
- **Cautions:**
  - The counts are informal: there is no trial table for the first two arms.
  - The last step confounds the phase timing with the speed command.
  - The comparator is an **immediate** stop, not the best clock-delayed stop at equal delay budget that CRR_READING.md names.
  - Stopping distance and stopping time are not reported.

### 8. A behavior-based framework for safe deployment of humanoid robots (Scianca, Ferrari, De Simone, Lanari & Oriolo)

- **Identifier:** Autonomous Robots 45:435-456 (2021), DOI 10.1007/s10514-021-09978-5, CC BY. Published-version PDF from the
  IRIS Sapienza repository.
- **Integrity:** `raw/scianca2021_iris.pdf` sha256 a69224bb81e995762db27b64244d4d018442e335fd0159b8fc43028a735a6fe8.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/scianca2021_iris.txt`

> Action: The robot will stop walking, ending in a double support configuration.

> The high-level velocity commands vx, vy, ω are immediately set to zero;

> The high-level velocity commands vx, vy, ω go from their current value to zero over a fixed arrest time;

> The arrest time used by stop is 2 s. As expected, the results indicate that with halt the robot stops immediately, almost bouncing back; whereas a much smoother finish is obtained using stop.

- **Reading: close P1** (r1a:10). This is the frontier's side, inside a safety framework for HRP-4 and NAO.
  - The emergency stop (halt) zeroes the velocity command at once and relies on the MPC gait generator's capturability
    terminal constraint, which is a state constraint.
  - The graceful stop ramps the command to zero over a **fixed arrest time on the clock** and ends in double support.
  - Both are shown triggered during double support only. There is no phase-keyed timing.

## C. The 2026 frontier: phase dependence stated, state-conditioned stops

### 9. Toward Certified Functional Safety for Industrial Humanoid Robots: The Fail-Passive Gap and a Feasibility Study (Ding, Cui, Wang & Wen, Siemens)

- **Identifier:** arXiv:2608.02809, **v1** (3 Aug 2026; the only version). Unitree G1. The stage-1 source r4:25, r4:28, r4:29
  and r4:32, re-fetched into this root.
- **Integrity:** `raw/2608.02809v1.pdf` sha256 75e18c76852b3463f16ecf1061fbb30389c2b3419e0303881b094aaf97ce3a07.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/2608.02809v1.txt`

> The safe stop is thus a constrained stop—minimize stopping time/distance while remaining within the capturable region— with no analog in ISO 13849 / EN 60204-1.

> Mid-step (single-support) demands. The outcome depends on gait phase. In double-support the support polygon is large and deceleration is quick; if the demand arrives in single-support (swing), immediate freezing is generally infeasible and the controller must place the current step (reach a capture point) before holding a static posture.

- **Reading: close P1** (r1a:11). The 2026 industrial safety analysis states that the stop's course depends on the gait phase
  (from swing, place the step first). It frames the safe stop as a constrained, capturability-bounded stop. But it designs no
  phase-keyed stop: the G1's own balancing policy performs the stop.

> (i) T is dominated by the uncertified reaction chain (tstop depends on the balancing policy and gait phase), so S can only be bounded using worst-case measured tstop;

> This imposes a phase-dependent lower bound on tstop, so the ISO 13855 distance must be sized using the worst-case (single-support) tstop.

- **Reading: contradicts P3** (r1a:12), for the current standards route only.
  - Under ISO 13855 (S = K·T + C), the separation distance takes one stopping time, the worst gait phase's, for every phase.
    This is the clock-sized stop that C-R1 replaces, written into the certification practice the paper follows.
  - It does not show that a draft such as ISO 25785-1 lacks phase-keyed timing. That text was not reachable.

### 10. Humanoid Safe Stop via Learned Stoppability Value (Long, Abbeel, Sreenath, Horowitz, Shi & Liu)

- **Identifier:** arXiv:2609.02358, **v1** (2 Sep 2026; the only version). Safe-Stop; Unitree G1.
- **Integrity:** `raw/2609.02358v1.pdf` sha256 e5e2bc9b1d2b89acf171e72fa9426dedb3a789c2192fa93ef719718cff407bdc.
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/2609.02358v1.txt`

> Humanoid robots responding to emergency stop commands typically execute a fixed maneuver, without reasoning about whether a safe stop is actually feasible from the current state.

> Most systems react by zeroing the velocity command and relying on the [...] motion controller to decelerate.

> This observation excludes the behavior-policy variables: motion phase, reference trajectory, global position, perceptual map, task command, base height, and base linear velocity.

> Second, there may be transient states from which immediate stopping appears unrecoverable, while continuing the behavior policy would return the robot to stoppable region. The current framework does not reason this.

- **Reading: close P1** (r1a:13).
  - A state-conditioned emergency stop: a learned stop policy, plus stoppability estimators that choose between the stop and a
    fall policy at the command. It deliberately **excludes the motion phase** from its inputs.
  - As a limitation, the authors name the **timing variant C-R1 predicts** (continue the gait until the state is stoppable,
    then stop), and say their framework does not do it.
  - Their description of common practice (zero the velocity command at once) is the phase-independent stop.

> We compare four rules: (a) stop-only, which always attempts πstop;

> the strict dual estimator gives the best safety, approving only 31 of 797 real failures.

- **Reading: close P2** (r1a:14).
  - A state-dependent choice at the stop command (attempt the stop, or hand off to a damping fall policy) is measured against
    always attempting the stop. It approves far fewer stops that end in a fall.
  - The conditioning is on proprioceptive state, not the gait phase. Stopping distance or time against a phase-independent
    stop is not reported.

## D. Standards proposals and industrial safety functions

### 11. A Pathway Study for Future Humanoid Standards (IEEE Humanoid Study Group; Prather, Keller et al.)

- **Identifier:** technical report, September 2025, DOI 10.13140/RG.2.2.27892.21122; copy hosted by The Robot Report (the
  stage-1 source r4:27 and r4:34).
- **Integrity:** `raw/IEEE-Humanoid-Report-of-Future-Standards-Development.pdf` sha256
  87f6a3418b5a40f764237d89074d76024de99152c3405a5faa96a1d2dcfa25d5 (identical to the harvester's copy).
- **Text:** `r1a/txt/IEEE-Humanoid-Report-of-Future-Standards-Development.txt`

> Level 0: Immediate power cut to all components.

> Level 3: Robot views surroundings, then plans and takes action to quickly and as safely as possible move to a safe robot state.

> Examples of such constraints are one-step-capturability and stability regions [2]. These constraints are based on calculating the regions around the robot during a foot swing phase in which a robot would be able to stabilize itself if it places its foot down within the region, and then to always ensure the robot can reach this region.

- **Reading: close P3** (r1a:15). The standards proposal:
  - grades e-stop behaviour from a power cut (Level 0) to a planned move to a safe state (Level 3);
  - names swing-phase capturability regions as a candidate minimum stability criterion for a standard;
  - sketches a double-stance, no-step mode near humans.

  It does not specify that the stop is timed or shaped by the gait phase.

## Is the mechanism published?

**P1: yes, since 1992-94.**
- Honda's emergency stop defers the stop until the biped's attitude over its support can hold it.
- AIST (2005) and Osaka (2006-07) shape the emergency stop of HRP-2 by the gait phase at the command: the landing time and
  position when the command comes in single support, an immediate stop in double support.
- The capture point (2006) chooses the stopping strategy from the state against the base of support.
- Cassie (ICRA 2023) defers a stand command to the apex phase.

**P2: once, on falls, against an immediate stop.** Crowley et al. report that the apex-timed stop took a stand transition from
under 10% success (immediate, phase-independent) to about 2 in 3, and to 100% of more than 20 trials with a speed adjustment.
Safe-Stop (2026) shows a state-conditioned (not phase-keyed) stop decision against always stopping.

**What was not found:**
- a comparison of a phase-keyed stop against the **best single clock-sized stop at equal delay budget**, over stop commands
  arriving uniformly in time, on **stopping distance or time** (worst case and mean). That is CRR_READING.md's stated
  prediction;
- a phase-keyed stop in any **standard, draft or deployed industrial function** that could be read. The standards route
  (ISO 13855 via Siemens 2026), Agility's Digit (a fixed time budget) and Boston Dynamics' Spot (a fixed timeout regardless of
  state) all budget the stop on the clock. The IEEE proposal names swing-phase capturability but not phase-keyed timing. The
  ISO 25785-1 draft was not reachable.

## Judgement calls, for the investigator's review

- **Timing against shaping.** The published designs split in two:
  - **shape the current step from any instant** (Morisawa, Takubo; the stop starts at once, and its landing time and place
    depend on the phase);
  - **defer the stop to a phase** (Honda; Cassie).

  Morisawa's introduction names waiting for the gait's own step boundary as the slow way to stop: earlier methods needed more
  than two steps. Cassie's deferral bought reliability, at the cost of waiting for the next apex phase. C-R1's prediction
  ("at equal delay budget") has to say which of the two it means. A gate would need both as baselines.
- **The worst case is bounded by physics.** Ding et al. state that the single-support stop has a phase-dependent lower
  bound: from swing, the step must be placed first. So the worst case over command times cannot fall below that bound,
  whatever the keying; at best it ties the stop sized by the worst phase. For the phase-deferring variant, the worst case
  adds the longest wait. Only the mean (and the fall rate) has obvious room. This bears on the "lower worst case" half of
  the prediction.
- **Capture point read as states.** The caller lists capture-point stopping as an instance of C-R1, so Pratt et al. are read
  **states**. A reviewer who takes C-R1 to mean an explicit gait-phase variable would read them **close**. P1 stays states on
  Honda, Morisawa, Tanaka, Takubo and Crowley either way.
- **Operational against safety stops.** Crowley (a stand command at the end of a run) and Peng (walk-to-stand) are operational
  stops, not safety functions. The mechanism (a stop command keyed to the phase) is the same. They are kept as states and close
  respectively.
- **Abstract-only sources.** Tanaka 2006 and Takubo 2007 rest on their abstracts (full text not reached), and Hase & Stein
  1998 on its MEDLINE abstract. Dropping all three changes no grade: P1 is carried by the full texts of Honda, Morisawa, Pratt
  and Crowley.
- **P3 readings.** "Contradicts" for sources 9, 13 and 14 means only that the documented standard or function budgets the stop
  on the clock. It cannot show that no draft or product keys the stop to the phase. The Honda and Agility patents are read
  **close** under the fixed policy, because a patent is not evidence of a deployed or certified function.
- **The window.** Honda (1994), Hase & Stein (1998), Morisawa (2005), Tanaka and Pratt (2006) and Takubo (2007) are older
  than 2015. They are kept because they state the mechanism in its foundational form.
