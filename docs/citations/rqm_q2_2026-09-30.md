## G1. Generative replay at the input level

### S1. Shin, Lee, Kim & Kim, "Continual Learning with Deep Generative Replay" (DGR)

- **Identifier:** arXiv:1705.08690, current version **v3** (12 Dec 2017; v1 24 May 2017, v2 9 Sep 2017); comments on the
  abstract page: "NIPS 2017". Fetched 2026-09-30.
- **Integrity:** PDF (v3) sha256 cd9cfc3c5b6e71fa3255a40037c6819f403415e2e4fcb539a83f9eda88067e10; abstract page sha256
  a3ae10e129f5ef4dab1181e475b2a7d3a192acb4f466ee849513dbb02b8891db.
- **Extracted text:** `1705.08690v3.txt`
- **Mechanism:** a GAN generator is trained sequentially (itself with replay) to mimic past inputs; generated inputs are
  labelled by the previous solver and interleaved with new data.
- **Stores:** generator and solver parameters; no data. **Backbone:** trained from scratch. **Compute:** not quantified.

> Although simply replaying all previous data alleviates the problem, it requires large memory and even worse, often infeasible in real world applications where the access to past data is limited.

> Called the scholar model, the generator-solver pair can produce fake data and desired target pairs as much as needed, and when presented with a new task, these produced pairs are interleaved with new data to update the generator and solver networks.

> The performance is therefore equivalent to joint training on accumulated real data as long as the generator recovers the input distribution.

> One defect of the generative replay framework is that the efficacy of the algorithm heavily depends on the quality of the generator.

- **Reading.** M5 **states** the quality limit ("heavily depends on the quality of the generator") and its converse, that an
  optimal generator gives joint training. The paper reports no standard class-IL score beside stored replay; those come from S2
  and S3.

### S4. van de Ven, Siegelmann & Tolias, "Brain-inspired replay for continual learning with artificial neural networks" (BI-R)

- **Identifier:** Nature Communications 11, Article 4069 (2020), doi 10.1038/s41467-020-17866-2, published online 2020-08-13
  (page metadata). HTML article page reached 2026-09-30 (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-17866-2). The PDF URL
  (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-17866-2.pdf) returned HTTP 429 three times: **PDF NOT REACHED**; the HTML full
  text was used. Europe PMC full text (PMC7426273) returned HTTP 503: NOT REACHED.
- **Integrity:** HTML sha256 de8cb4f51a061e2d464e6f5481600d9a471f173451b0ffdde829561e09e8ae18.
- **Extracted text:** `bir.txt`
- **Mechanism:** a VAE merged into the classifier ("replay through feedback"), conditional, context-gated, replaying
  hidden representations after frozen early layers, with distillation targets.
- **Stores:** model parameters (generator folded into the network); no data. **Backbone:** MNIST protocols from scratch;
  CIFAR-100 with convolutional layers pre-trained on CIFAR-10 and frozen. **Numbers:** in figures only on the reached page;
  not quoted.

> However, scaling up generative replay to complicated problems with many tasks or complex inputs is challenging.

> Our method achieves state-of-the-art performance on challenging continual learning benchmarks (e.g., class-incremental learning on CIFAR-100) without storing data, and it provides a novel model for replay in the brain.

> It is true that for a naive implementation of GR, in which full pseudo-datasets are generated for all previous tasks and concatenated to the current task’s training set, this concern certainly applies. Importantly, however, using replay does not necessarily mean fully retraining on all previous tasks. For example, for the implementation of replay used in this paper, in each iteration only a fixed amount of samples were replayed.

> we report experiments on the MNIST dataset highlighting the surprising efficiency and robustness of replay: replaying just a few or low-quality samples can already be enough.

> we found that a straight-forward implementation of GR did not scale well to such a long series of tasks, as its performance rapidly declined after about 15 tasks

> To simulate development, we pre-trained the convolutional layers of our model on CIFAR-10, a dataset containing similar but non-overlapping images compared to CIFAR-100 35 . During the incremental training on CIFAR-100, those convolutional layers were frozen and we replayed only through the fully connected layers.

> Only translating the internal replay component to other input modalities will not be straight-forward, as this one depends on pre-trained convolutional layers.

> Another drawback of internal replay is that the rigid, pre-trained convolutional layers likely restrict the ability of the model to learn out-of-distribution inputs

- **Reading.** M5 **states** the scaling limit (many tasks, complex inputs) and **bears** on compute (a fixed replay budget per
  iteration keeps the cost independent of the number of tasks). M1 **close**: the feature-level variant that works on
  CIFAR-100 needs frozen pre-trained early layers. M7 **bears**: from scratch, only the MNIST protocols were run. The drawbacks
  quoted are the paper's own limitations for other modalities.
